akesi linja

kowaisoho (kowo+aiso+ho) is the word used for "snake." It can refer to actual snakes (kikitanq kowaisohlóõ (I hate snakes)) or 'snakes' in the figurative sense, like how we use rat in English slang - 'kowaisohychsubekã à' (all snakes!  - referring to people as sly, backstabbers, or betrayers). You can use it as a verb, in which it means slither, like 'soa kowaisofu' (he slithered (like a snake)). The verbal meaning can also be figurative, as in betray or backstab. kowaisoho can also be used as a vulgar or crude word for a penis. kowaisohot is the negative form (backstab, betray), and kowaisohóy would be the more 'positive' form (sneaky, sly). Though calling your friend "kowaisoho" would be quite rude, so the positive form is mandatory, plus using another word is highly encouraged - 'lilikowaisohóy' or 'sawakowaisohóy' (little sneaky (person) or sweet and cunning respectively). It can also refer to kebab or any item that looks like a snake. Unfortunately, it can also mean poop or turd if implied, and this is generally rude to do, since kowaisoho is also a food term. So be cautious when using kowaisoho as a slang for poop or turd.


pana e telo

from ma (three drops of water) + ku (stand, rise, establishment), it represents water flowing down a standing person. It refers to crying, sobbing, or severe emotional distress (mamaku / makunyi). Shedding a tear or the lowest form is makusta. it can also refer to pleas, pleading, or 'crying' for help. Depending on how severe it is, maku, mamaku, makunyi, or mamakunyi are used to specify.


waso telo

from ka (shell, first) + ngi (bird), this refers to wild ducks. so if it's in a pond, it's a kangi. more specifically, it can refer to mallards. When used as an adjective, it can be slightly derogatory or cheeky and playful. So calling someone kangi means that they're gullible or easy to defeat. When used with positive connotations, as in kangioy, it can mean beautiful, cute, or cuddly, though you can also use kanginq for that. Kangi also refers to winter season. When used to describe winter, especially in poetry, it sets the scene for a solemn, cold, quiet, and reflective imagery.


waso telo pi ala

from kangi + nq (or aiso+kan+gi), this refers to domesticated ducks. so if it's in a farm or house, it's a kanginq. more specifically, it can refer to diving ducks. When used as an adjective, it means cute, beautiful, fluffy, or cuddly. So calling someone kanginq means that they're beautiful, cute, or cuddly. Kanginq also refers to summer season. When used to describe summer, it's imagery contains slice-of-life, warm, emotional, and calm or joyous scenery. The standard Tawish word is "kanginq," but the Modern kanji version is "aisokangi." Only kanginq is important or mandatory to learn.


duckphoria

from kangi (duck) + ((n)ata(se)), kangata refers to the happiness derived from a duck. it is when you see a duck and become duckhappy. The formal word is kanginirunaiqe (archaic or literary). kangataqe is also an acceptable form, meaning the emotion felt from duckphoria itself. so it refer to the feeling of duckphoria. Very meta!


pilin wile kama

Ushikitsu possesses a semantic space primarily centered on the concept of enjoyment, fun, and pleasure, but it extends further to describe pleasant experiences and, in related forms, anticipation of the future. It is a broad, subjective adjective describing an experience or state, rather than just a momentary feeling. For example, "fun time" is "ushikitsu(ã) wano". Heartwarming or comfort is also ushikitsu, like "sa ni ushikitsua" (she is heartwarming / ~my comfort-person). As a verb, ushikitsu is about taking pleasure in or enjoying, like in "ushikitsuru wanosun jiteisanji" (I enjoyed my time at her place). When passive, it can mean relief, comfort, or easy, like in "su ni ushikitsunai" (I am relieved). It can also be used to describe excitedness, like in 'ushikitsu kamu" (to eat happily / excitedly). you can also use it for "looking forward to," like in "Ushikitsureu kangiwano" (~looking forward to winter season).

musi ala

Koibwu is the stereotypical "bored" word. you say it when there's nothing to do, you say it when you're fed up, etc. to better convey this, use the particles -sta, -nyi, and -ie as well as reduplication. Kokoibwu refers to exhaustion, tiredness, and koibwunyi refers to lethargy, apathy, burdensome lack of motivation. Kokoibwunyi would be the ultimate and most strong form, referring something like a total loss of the will to live itself, but context would convey the meaning. In its diminutive forms, as koibwusta, it simply refers to a simple "eh, nothing to do" remark. It can also refer to dryness (as in dry people) or blandness. Repetitiveness or overexposure is also koibwu.

pilin pona mute

Maracata (from maraca (to soothe) + ata (happy)) refers to the happiness that soothing or pleasuring yields.  It's other form, maracanyata (maraca+nyu+ata) is used for orgasms or sexual pleasure. The mamaracata and maracatasta forms are used for the superlative (disorienting euphoria, cruising the bliss sea, overwhelming ecstasy) and diminutive (relaxed, soothed) respectively. Why not just use kanata? Maracata implies that the happiness was reached through some sort of relaxing, soothing, or something else, whereas kanata does not. Compare: "so ni o nuku maracata" (he soothed me / relaxed me into joy) vs. "so ni o nuku kanata" (he made me happy). Superlative and diminutive form exist for the sexual "maracanyata" as well. It can also refer to ejaculation if used with the proper case / grammar or in the proper context.

pakala li pini

hwogimi (from woi + hyo + go + mi) refers to mending something or filling a hole at the core. it is when something missing is made hole again. for example, 'kanatanq honcha ni sua hwogimi' (I helped the sad girl feel better). Hwogimi alone does not have sexual connotations, but as hwogiminyu, it might. Hwogimista is a diminutive form, meaning "patch (up / together)" or "stitch." It's different from hwogimi in that patching something up can be rough but mending something isn't. Some tape over a leaking bottle is patching it up, but it does not mean it'll hold. Hwohwogimi would be a superlative, meaning something along the lines of "complete." For monetary or financial compensation, hwaregimi is used (from woi + hyo + (k)are + go + mi).

tame pi len sijelo

it refers to skin cancer. it's made from re (inside, middle) + awa (skin, hide) + uru (body, meat) + iki (village). Uruiki refers to heaviness, but it means swollen / tumour here. Rewa means inside the skin. Together, rewauruiki refers to mesothelioma.

pakala lon sijelo

haginkiswo ('a+gyo+hin+iki+swo) refers to any abnormal or non-normal growth anywhere. it can be the spreading of harmful rhetoric through a society (a tumour of society) or it can be malignant cancer. For negatives, usually the haginkiswot form is used (malignant, cancer, harmful). For positives, the haginkiswoy form is used; however, haginkiswoy often does not refer to benign tumours, since those aren't really 'good' or positive. For that, you'll use something like haginkiswotasnh (~benign tumour, harmless growth, useless swelling). haginkiswo can also refer to inflammation, both inside (haginkiswoji) something or outside (haginkiswojinh) something.

kiwen linja

Refers to any bone or bone-like object. Can refer to phalluses or phallic-like structures as well (umairinya), especially if erect. Umiarinya is the erotic form. Otherwise, this refers to anatomical bones.

suli, lawa, lon

ongutsu refers to the innermost source from which everything is emergent. For example, you would use Zhamungutsu to describe the root essence of God (like Dhatiyyah in Islam), as opposed to Zhamushí (a God particle; e.g., Higgs boson) or Zhamusumatà (God-ness, God in Himself). Someone with hope has ongutsu. Ongutsu is also metaphorical, like in "Nameku iinq no ongutsulu" (prune the roots of evil). It can also refer to basis, like 'itagiryã no ongutsu' (this idea's basis). It differs from shí in that shí refers to an indivisible, smallest conceptual entity, and it differs from sumatà in that sumatà is the existence of that thing as a quality, like -ness or -ity in goodness or vitality. Ongutsu is the fabric, from which X emerges. So hongutsu is soul, whereas honsumatà is humanity and honshí primordial follicle or sperm. The possibilities are endless, since -ongutsu is commonly a compounding word.

akesi lawa

a cowarin is a louse. Cowaringu refers to parasites as a whole. A poki cowarin would be a leech. Cowarin can also be used to refer to people who "leech" off of others or behave like parasites and contribute nothing to society. It can refer to freeloaders too. Cowarin is not a vulgar term. As a verb, it means to suck blood, both literally and metaphorically (suck the colour / life out (of)).

supa waso pi jan sewi

from hajira (holy) + eme (wings), this refers to the wings of an angel. It is often used for very pious people - 'soa au hajireme' (he has angel wings).

lon noka

its direct opposite is urukunq (sit). Uruku means to stand and its semantic space encompasses anything from erecting a structure to a hoisting apparatus. Stalls, lemonade stands, kiosks - these are all uruku. Being useful is also uruku (urukuthu aiwumbo - stood for their help), like 'standing in X role.' you can also use it for point of view, like 'urukuson ni na′a subĩ uruku liliasaiemyãõ' (~He believes that we should built more little homes).

potato, pan

horochi refers to potatoes. It comes from hó (the radical for all starchy or potato-related stems) and rochi, which refers to starchy staple foods, like bread or potatoes.

isi pi sona ala, toki pi nasin sona, wile sona

Kyunso refers to a simple proposition or supposition. In its most simplest meaning, it means 'hypothesise.' It can be inflected as a verb (kyunsu) to mean 'hypothesise.' To say conjecture or speculation, you say kyunssta (kyunsuta in Koyeah Tawa). To say thesis, you say kykyunso or kyunshimg. Use kyunshimg if its a highly systematic, complex, and developed model of its own; e.g., string theory is kyunshimg, but some random student's thesis probably isn't (it would be kykyunso). To say theory, you say kyunsonyi. To say law (constant observation, proven), you say kyunshimgmei (alive model).

pilin, pilin pona, pilin ike

makure is an umbrella term for ataingu (desideratives). So for example, atai covers desideratives, both as a suffix and as an independently marked root. makure refers to "inner pains" or "inner tortures" since that's what it means literally. Maku means wail and sob (painfully) and re refers to the middle or innerness of something. In some situations though, other derivations will be more favourable than simply using makure. It is a highly poetic and literary term, used in proverbs like "makurezun a rrahwo i shi riwakasarunai" (your trials amount to negation if you keep on thinking).

tomo tawa pi nasin kiwen

Rwankaden is formed from rwan (rain), ka (rice paddy, field), and den (cart, car). It refers to trains broadly.

ilo toki

from rwanka (electric) + uta (mouth), rwankuta refers to 'phone.'

ijo li lon sama lon tu

from the ari (wild, upside-down box) and umi (crown, superficial, superfluous) radicals, this compound (ariumi) literally means "wild superficiality / indeterminate superfluous state." It can refer to quantum decoherence as 'ariǵumi' (known superficial state), where ari is reversed. Ariumiǵ, though, refers to wavefunction collapse. Figuratively, as ariumiku, it can refer to being at two positions at once, especially in idiomatic usage: "su ni ariumiku no miri ue" (I'm stuck between two places / I don't know what to do / caught in a sticky situation/decision). Literally, (ariumikumg), it may refer to something being at two places: sa a toariumikumg ko ue (she was at two places yesterday).

ma, kasi ale, ale

Mama refers to nature. Whilst it isn't inherently its own term, as it is formed by the reduplication of the ma radical (meaning 'to flow' / symbolising three drops of water), it has been lexicalised. It literally means: 'the highest flow / the most flowing (of water).' It refers to mother nature and is a beautiful term. You'll often see it in poetic expressions, like mama no hataka (face of nature), which refers to awe-inspiring scenery, or mama no bareku (nature's blessings), which can refer to the aroma of the plants, the life-saving chemicals of flowers, and the bird's song in the morning, like her (mama) reminding you to wake up. Originally, mama came from the English term "mama" (mother) and referred to nature as a mother (since in Tawa, you'd say mimi for mother and kari for father). Though kari just means 'parent,' it often refers to the father.

self-1

refers to the self: ansu -> the self that acts; onsu -> the self that experiences / is; omgsu -> the subjugated self / bound self with no control; amgsu -> the self that chooses to experience; mansu -> the self that strives for change/maturity; monsu -> the self that has matured; momgsu -> the self that matured because it had to (tragic/passive); mamgsu -> the self that chose maturity. As ijensu, it refers to ancestors, and as echinsu, it refers to descendants / progeny.

rust-1

Hoserebo literally means 'starchy silver,' referring to the 'flakes' or 'decomposed starch' of silver. It refers to rust in practice: 'Rwankageo a tofiyui subeko no hoserebōe tahetesun dan' (the engineer removed all of the rust from my electronic).

tech-1

It refers to a "tech," like an engineer or mechanic. Daerwankageo would be the formal form, so probably an engineer/mechanic.

gear-1

Ibaubakang literally means 'to make orbit circularly' or 'to spin.' In its formal form, it can refer to the gears of a machine (daeibaubakang) but otherwise it refers to spinners generally, even fidget spinners as ukibaubakang (child's spinner / toy spinner).

food-root-1

Ikamebang refers to kitchen, literally mean 'place of preparing food.' It is different from ikamenebang, which refers to the place where food is made edible (e.g., depending on context, could be the top of the stove, inside of an oven, etc.). There are more words with kamo/chi and the kanji affixes: Kameba / chieba - food/water storage; Ikamung - cook (specifically referring to making something eatable); Ikamong - cook (making food, doesn't have a heating/cooking connotation); Ingkamu - utensils; Ingikamung - stove; Ingikamong - cutting board, knife, seasoning, topping, etc. Kamina - food disorder; Kamenina - eating disorder; Kamenaiina - autocannibalistic disorder; Kamnyina - fetishistic food disorder; Kamnyenina - fetishistic eating disorder; Kamnyenaiina - fetishistic autocannibalistic disorder; Disorder/illness/sickness can be differentiated with dae- (disorder), so someone with kamina might have food poisoning, but someone with daekamina has a medical disorder pertaining to food.Ocekamina - food rejection disorder; Ocekamenina - eating rejection disorder (e.g., vomiting disorders); Kamugeo - professional eater / food critic; Kampan - food bread (sandwich, roti, etc.); Kanden - food car (e.g., food truck); Kamgyo - meat; these aren't concrete lexicalised forms, they are just formed from Kanji compounding.

school-1

Insuebang refers to a place where one's self is prepared. This is split into many forms: iansuebang (where the acting self matures), ionsuebang (where the experiencing self matures), iamgsuebang (where the self that chose to experience matures), iomgsuebang (where the self that never wanted to experience matures). Then with the flow forms: imansuebang (where the self that strives for maturity is prepared), imonsuebang (where the already matured self further develops), imomgsuebang (where the self that matured because it had to further matures), and iamgsuebang (where the self that chose to mature matures). Iansuebang refers to school, ionsuebang refers to experience/intuition/life/trials, iamgsuebang refers mosques/masajid or places of worship, and iomgsuebang refers to jobs, challenges, or situations one never wanted to be in, or the pains or troubles of life.

robot-1

Rwankahon refers to robots or anything that is merely humanoid. e.g., rwankahoni fofen can be used to describe speech that is dry and robotic.

ac-1

Ingireteng refers to a cooler (air conditioner). Ingireteṋ / inginashitang is used for heater.

window-1

It refers to anything carrying light, like a bulb. As inghinjeru (to put light in), it specifically refers to windows, but as a verb. To use it as a noun, you should use it in the gerund form inghinjen.

flash-1

Ihing refers to making light. Ingihing would refer to 'tool of making light,' such as a flashlight. For photosynthesis, you'd use 'ihingun' (making through light / making via light). Postpositions can function as suffixes like -un when in radical constructions.

cr-1

refers to a place of study, like a classroom (daepayaeba).

component-1

refers to the interior or layout of something; e.g., Muzumu no monore (the museum's interior / layout).

door-1

ing- (tool), gen- (through), and -eba (room) means door, since it refers to tools that let you go across rooms. Whenever the word starts with -g, the g in ing is not omitted, so ing + gana = inggana. The rule is only for /k/, where ing + kana = inkana.

love-2

Mātase (spelt) literally means "to be happy due to flowing / crying." Ma refers to the three water droplets, which represent: flow, vitality, and soul / inner cries. It is an extremely direct and intimate word for love. It is like saying 'my soul is overflowing so much that my cries have turned into joy' or 'my vitality is only there because of our flow and your soul.' Example: "Mātaseru" (I love you). It's highly direct and deeply emotional. This is a lexicalised term. Here, -ru is used in its archaic meaning (nakedly / vulnerably). You never say mātasenyu, because 'nyu' (romantic suffix) overcrowds the word and destroys the 'love' element. -ru means "nakedly / vulnerably" (lexicalised archaic) only when used with specific verbs. Mātase is an example. On such verbs, -ru signifies the true, raw, stripped-back, and/or "naked" nature/truth.

akesi linja

kowaisoho (kowo+aiso+ho) is the word used for "snake." It can refer to actual snakes (kikitanq kowaisohlóõ (I hate snakes)) or 'snakes' in the figurative sense, like how we use rat in English slang - 'kowaisohychsubekã à' (all snakes!  - referring to people as sly, backstabbers, or betrayers). You can use it as a verb, in which it means slither, like 'soa kowaisofu' (he slithered (like a snake)). The verbal meaning can also be figurative, as in betray or backstab. kowaisoho can also be used as a vulgar or crude word for a penis. kowaisohot is the negative form (backstab, betray), and kowaisohóy would be the more 'positive' form (sneaky, sly). Though calling your friend "kowaisoho" would be quite rude, so the positive form is mandatory, plus using another word is highly encouraged - 'lilikowaisohóy' or 'sawakowaisohóy' (little sneaky (person) or sweet and cunning respectively). It can also refer to kebab or any item that looks like a snake. Unfortunately, it can also mean poop or turd if implied, and this is generally rude to do, since kowaisoho is also a food term. So be cautious when using kowaisoho as a slang for poop or turd.


pana e telo

from ma (three drops of water) + ku (stand, rise, establishment), it represents water flowing down a standing person. It refers to crying, sobbing, or severe emotional distress (mamaku / makunyi). Shedding a tear or the lowest form is makusta. it can also refer to pleas, pleading, or 'crying' for help. Depending on how severe it is, maku, mamaku, makunyi, or mamakunyi are used to specify.


waso telo

from ka (shell, first) + ngi (bird), this refers to wild ducks. so if it's in a pond, it's a kangi. more specifically, it can refer to mallards. When used as an adjective, it can be slightly derogatory or cheeky and playful. So calling someone kangi means that they're gullible or easy to defeat. When used with positive connotations, as in kangioy, it can mean beautiful, cute, or cuddly, though you can also use kanginq for that. Kangi also refers to winter season. When used to describe winter, especially in poetry, it sets the scene for a solemn, cold, quiet, and reflective imagery.


waso telo pi ala

from kangi + nq (or aiso+kan+gi), this refers to domesticated ducks. so if it's in a farm or house, it's a kanginq. more specifically, it can refer to diving ducks. When used as an adjective, it means cute, beautiful, fluffy, or cuddly. So calling someone kanginq means that they're beautiful, cute, or cuddly. Kanginq also refers to summer season. When used to describe summer, it's imagery contains slice-of-life, warm, emotional, and calm or joyous scenery. The standard Tawish word is "kanginq," but the Modern kanji version is "aisokangi." Only kanginq is important or mandatory to learn.


duckphoria

from kangi (duck) + ((n)ata(se)), kangata refers to the happiness derived from a duck. it is when you see a duck and become duckhappy. The formal word is kanginirunaiqe (archaic or literary). kangataqe is also an acceptable form, meaning the emotion felt from duckphoria itself. so it refer to the feeling of duckphoria. Very meta!

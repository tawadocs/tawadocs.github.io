unpa e mi, musi e sijelo mi

borrowed directly from Gyakyyi, which loaned it from older Takan languages. The word's origin was in Kawakanamagana, which loaned it from Greek, in which it meant to masturbate. Maraca in Modern Tawa simply refers to any act of pleasure or pleasing something or oneself. It could be physical, sensory pleasure, such as warm baths, or it could be emotional pleasure, derived through touch or song. When made romantic, as in maracanyu, it carries a sexual connotation. Since it is not mandatory for maraca to carry -(r)u or -nyu, often times, the context disambiguates if maraca is being used romantically or platonically. Explicitly marking it romantic, though, as in maracanyu / maracat, makes it sexual too. When sexual, it is a pretty vulgar word, referring to sexual stimulation, masturbation, or fondling one's genitals (touching oneself). It can refer to orgasms too (~maracanyata) or ecstatic feelings (~maracata). In the lower forms, such as maracasta, it can refer to self-care or doing something to feel better. Like, 'Kamteikikitãsun ni o maracastanai' (~my favourite food soothed me a little). Maracat would be soothing with an ulterior motive and maracaoy would be soothing positively - esp. in the context of people-pleasing and as such. Do note that maracat can also be used for sexual pleasure, as in "Kim Jong Uun ni maracanaitmas no komuni" (Kim Jong Un is master of goon). (maracanait = masturbate, preferred form (as opposed to maracatnai)).

pakala e telo, kama tan poki

it refers to anything from emotional slips (when you can't help but smile and it shows, as it is genuine, even though you don't want it to) to literal liquid spills, like in the idiomatic expression 'chisuno taranai' (my water has been spilled). It can imply agency in chisuna taranai (my water has been spilled (due to me)) or when paired with 'ni,' the focus could be on the state (of being spilled) itself. Tara can also refer to leaking something private, such as secrets, or flaws in boundaries. This 'flaw' can be literal (a bottle with a little hole) or more abstract (a relationship without proper privacy). Note: tara is not in the core lexicon because this can technically be boiled down to a core word.

bopu is a word used to describe anything short of a breast. as a verb, it can refer to lactation, and as a noun, it refers to any milk-producing organ in a living thing, including udders and tits. It's not inherently a vulgar word but it can be if used casually or randomly. It also describes bouncy, breast-like, or saggy things (daminq bopu) depending on context, though this essentially like saying 'that looks like a saggy breast' - probably not something you'd use in normal daily conversation, unless you want to be called a shifumo.


sewi telo

Sorama is a newer term, as opposed to Sorachi. Soramaru would be to rain, whereas formally or poetically it is 'sorala chiru' (the sky waters). These are the other -ma weathers: reretema (hail), retema (cold weather), nashitama (heatwaves, hot weather), kiukoma (windy, turbulent), bakama (tornado), chimama (tsunami), sowma (sunny), meiyama (cloudy), reteshima (snowy), damima (mild, clear, temperate), kamama (low visibility, dark, foggy), mamama (stormy), tahetema (thunderstorm), pokichima (typhoon/hurricane), rereteshima (blizzard), sosowma (drought), reretemama (severe, intense, big hail). Do note that each can further derive even more roots with derivational particles: tahetemaoy (nice thunderstorm), tahetemat (unpleasant thunderstorm), mamamata (happy storm / a storm of happy emotion), etc. And, of course, you would use soramangu for "weather" itself.


toki, toki ante

Gana refers to language. Ganairime would be linguistics, Ganaumashí could be sociolinguistics, Ganashí would be a phoneme. Ganangutsu is... well, a mystery. So depending on context, ganangutsu would refer to a specific theory of the root of language. Ganasumatà would be the essence of language - a unique human method of communication via thoughts and symbols. And so on. Gana comes from the Kawakanamagana word for 'language.' In many contexts, gana can also refer to grammar (like gana ribu - grammar book), though you can use 'ganatachi' or ganatachitei for grammar.


toki ante

loaned from the Takan languages, agona refers to a dialect. It is a different way of speaking. Agonanya would be a gynolect (female genderlect) and agonanye would be androlect (male genderlect). The derivation is similar, so you can derive many technical concepts with this.


sewi, jan sewi

General filler word. It doesn't refer to God Himself but, rather, any deity that is like God. It's a tame, mild word. Zhamu is also used in poetic constructions, such as 'janà no zhamunya' (goddess from heaven (romantic)). It's also used in the formal greeting 'are zhamua barekunai zuõ' (may God bless you, where 'are' marks the wish / hope (may - optative)). The word 'gopo' is rarely used as a replacement by speakers that have trouble pronouncing zhamu.


seme

pan, pan laso, pan loje

Used for all bread-related roots. Jireipan refers to naan, napipan refers to baguettes, etc. One irregular term is 'napii-samipan,' literally meaning 'long fish bread,' but it refers to croissants.


mama, mama meli

Used for "mother." Can modify other nouns like mimii mama (Mother nature).

rest-1

Used for 'rest of' or 'others.' Like 'arenki hon' means 'the rest of the people / the remaining people / the other people.' It is a casual contraction of arenekui. As an adverb in the arenkinu form, it means 'conversely / on the other hand.' Even though it is a contraction, saying arenei IS DIFFERENT. "Arenei hon" would mean different person, NEVER rest of / remaining people in THIS sense.


cross-1

fox-1

Tomoe refers to foxes. They are intelligent, sometimes sly and cunning, animals that live long lives. It is a garibankana, so the 'e' ending is swapped for the appropriate affix. In the noun and accusative form, it is tomōe, in the modifier form, it is tomoi, and as an adverb modifier it is tomoinu. Someone who is like a tomoe is likely healthy but also clever. Tomota would be negative (deceitful), tomooi refers to a positive connotation, so an intelligent, handsome, or healthy individual. Tomota can also describe façades - beautiful from the outside, but a different story inside; e.g., 'irimeson ni watada tomotare ue' (his work is merely a façade (facade) inside / all fluff no substance / deceitful). Tomotare is usually used for façade. Tomooire refers to the opposite - a non-façade, if you will, something that is how it looks. If it's beautiful outside, it's healthy inside. So a delicious pizza might be tomooire. But... you probably wouldn't use this for pizza. You'd use it like: 'rra, reoeanate tomooire woui newōe!' (no, we will be going to a store that gives exactly what it promises / will be going to a store that is reliable / will be going to a trustworthy store). To describe a spiritually healthy person: "nukore ga tomooii ue" (his inner heart is still like a fox (spiritually well)). For physical unwellness, you'd make other comparisons: "hyakoson ga mahwai ue" (his clothes are unwell / he is not hygienic). Someone who is echitomota (post-fox) is a wise / elder fox, as he has learnt. A tomota (deceitful/immature fox) is immature / unwise as a person. It can also show stealth: "so a tomoinu hateru usehon do" (he stealthily ran through the group of people). A direct antonym to the tomoe would be angtomoe (foxless / fox-spiritless). It refers to someone who may have studied everything on a textbook but still gets fooled by a simple scam. To put one into a façade / trick someone is tomotajeru. A tomōho (lit. "fox-spoon" but refers to the tail of the fox) is the most sensitive or revealling feature of the person, like a flaw in a flawless façade or a slip of the tongue. "Niru tomōhōe" (I see your fox's tail) means 'I know exactly what you are trying to do, and it won't fly!').


spirit-1

Yōkai refers to spirits and 'beings of the essence,' but it is more of a conversational term, as Tawish culture only recognises God at the top with no one else like Him.  It is also used metaphorically to mean 'spirit' as in alcohol or liquids that evapourate fast. It can refer to good odours and ointments as yodaekai (formal spirit). It is a garibankana, so all affixes are inserted into the double oo slot (yOOkai). So yōkai + ta = yotakai; yōkai + oi = yooikai, etc.


congrats-1

Loaned from "Mazeltov / Mazel tov," the Jewish expression. Mazetoven is used to express 'well done! / greatly executed! / that's amazing!' so after the end of a good prank call, you'd exclaimed 'mazetoven a!' or 'mazetoven!'


love-2

Mātase (spelt) literally means "to be happy due to flowing / crying." Ma refers to the three water droplets, which represent: flow, vitality, and soul / inner cries. It is an extremely direct and intimate word for love. It is like saying 'my soul is overflowing so much that my cries have turned into joy' or 'my vitality is only there because of our flow and your soul.' Example: "Mātaseru" (I love you). It's highly direct and deeply emotional. This is a lexicalised term. Here, -ru is used in its archaic meaning (nakedly / vulnerably). You never say mātasenyu, because 'nyu' (romantic suffix) overcrowds the word and destroys the 'love' element. -ru means "nakedly / vulnerably" (lexicalised archaic) only when used with specific verbs. Mātase is an example.


gender-1

It refers to gender, lexicalised from gi + ngu (essential lexical category). If you wanted to say abstract nouns, you'd use gingo, though. rungu and nyungu work (but platonic and romantic difference is baked into the terms respectively). For infinitives' category, you say tengu. If you wanted to say infinitive, you'd likely use tenga (the part of an infinitive or tengo for the whole infinitive). Gingu refers to someone's essential sex, so insulting it can be super rude and vulgar.

filler-20

used like a softener. It often means 'isn't it?' with the proper tone or particles, like 'ko, zu a reoeanai anē?' (tomorrow, you'll be going, right?) It can also be used to grab someone's attention, like a soft 'yo/hey.' Also used in constructions like 'hakinu anē?' (really, is that so?) which are often sarcastic; e.g., "the bird flew" - "hakinu anē" (really? wow! / yeah, they tend to do that).


filler-11

Used in sentences like "seka ue" (It may be that way). As an attributive (sekai), it has a 'maybe/possibly' nuance, like "sekai... tokedeswai..." (maybe it is true that I really was betrayed). With sekai-fui, it creates a little oxymoron "maybe-really." If you said "sekai-fui ue!!", it would be like saying "I really hope that's true!!" / "It may not be true but I sure hope it is!" At the end of the sentence, it can express regret, especially with -reta (sekareta); e.g., "are tokamu sekareta" (I wish that I did eat it, though). That could mean: I wish that I did eat it (I didn't, though I could have) or I wish that I did eat it (sadly, it did not happen / I could not because it didn't exist or I was just thinking about it). favour-1

It literally means 'to favour / prefer,' like in: "Angaoesun" (without my preference / not preferable). As a verb: "ryo ou aoeru" (I prefer / favour this) or "ryōe ou angaoeru" (I dislike this / I do not prefer this). It is quite a formal word but it is also highly polite when used instead of 'no;' e.g., 'hiou ga angaoei ue' (that is not preferable / that is not to my liking). The potential form is used like this: "Aoetasu" (I can prefer it); e.g., "Ryōe ou aoetasu" (I can like this). As an attributive: "Zuno ga aoetashieoi ue" (I could see myself liking you / I could take a liking to you). Or just "Reoaoetashieoi ue" (I could see myself taking a liking to you later on)  (reo- was used for imperfective aspect). It can mean a literal favour too: "Teqeka yo? O aoenai!" - literally, it's like saying "Remember? (I am) favoured," but it really means "remember? you owe me a favour." Tawa hates constructions like "Zu a sunōe ou aoeru" (you favour me / you owe me a favour), so it is ALWAYS more natural and fluent to completely rephrase. If you really wanted to make it explicit: "Teqeka yo? O anishite aoenai" can be used (Remember? I must be favoured / Remember? You owe me a favour).


hard-3

"Uuwa" means hard or difficult; e.g., "Uuwai ue!" (it's hard!). It is the modern term used instead of damiṋ/daminq.

A 'masculine', casual, and 'tough' word for friend. Calling someone 'tengu' is like saying 'little one / buddy / boy.' For example: "Ritaroe mo haeku, tengu" (walk gently, little one / walk with care, little one). It sounds the same as the word for verbals/infinitive (category) (tengu, from te- + -ngu), which is very different.


above-3

Modern term for above (as opposed to archaic nandaṋ). e.g., asa no ia ou jiru (put it above the house / put it on the roof of the house). Asa no nanda ou jiru (place it below the house).

A polite form of hatakaṋ. Hatakaṋ is used for both butt/ass and behind. You never want to use that word in a respectful setting, so you opt for oucao; e.g., "Oucaōe no ng" (the thing behind (you) / the one behind), "Oucai asa" (the house behind), "oucaizun no asa ita fofen" (I'm talking about the house that's behind you).


slangfiller-1

Like 'shit / stuff' informally in English; e.g., 'fapechi kiuōe' (some Orwellian shit / some dystopian type stuff). 'Kiuo yoe, ii riwakango ue!' (on some real shit, you got a good brain (i.e., you're smart)). 'Kiuo yoe n... shiou, otōe ue?' (holy shit... WHAT is that?) - note that the demonstrative 'shiou' (that) refers to artificial / human made objects. So the speaker may be referring to a weird sculpture or bizarre object.


interj-3, okay

okie is a complementising particle. It is added before a phrase in the okiete infinitive form but after as okie alone. example: "Ochada n, iou yawa a kusuchei i okie." (Well, those guys were always going to act like shitheads / I should've known, they were always pieces of shit.) Kusuche is the masculine platonic form of kusu (shit/fuck). It is especially vulgar here due to the presence of the expletive n after ochada n, which makes the whole sentence much more angry and harsh than it sounds in English. Okie always carries a "I should have expected" tone. Okieoi (in any case, I should have known it was positive) / okieta (still, I should have expected it to be bad); e.g., "Iou hon ga fui dakatamashi ue... okieta" (that guy is actually a thief... and in any case, I should've known that it would turn out to be bad / anyway, he was always bad, apparently). Context, tone, other particles, etc. do the work. If you want to remove the "... should have expected" tone, you say "okie seka ue" (in any case, it turned out to be that way). This one implies that you weren't expecting it.


percentage-thing-1

Represents the percent (%). For example: "mani no mijou juka ji ryōe ou subu" (I want this item for 50% of the original price).


sev1-2

with connotations, space is controllable: uzunta (severely) vs. uzumi (far too / way too). Usually meliorative by default.


gradual-2

Describes gradual change; e.g., "safuki yara" (to become X gradually). It can also mean slowly or carefully: "Safukōe mo haeku" (walk with care). Usually, the noun form (safukōe) is used for this, plus a germane postposition.


extent-3

extent / until / boundary. Used in the construction: "hiou euma da kamen, reopokii ue" (if you continue to eat, you'll become fatter and fatter).


intj-2, so-1

Often a filler, especially if lengthened: "ddoooooo, hiou no ottan momg sano ga mafi ue" (soooo... it is her that is mad because of this reason). Though, this sentence is ambiguous, as it could mean "she is blushing (because of that reason)." You may use mafita/mafioi for disambiguation, but it is generally not needed. It functions like a "so" connective (ddoe): "ddoe suno a tokamu, anee, ddoe sono wa kamu" (so, I ate, (right?), so he eats as well). Head-final form is ddo; e.g., "mahwasun momg kamu-kamu ddoh" (because of my illness, I had no choice to do anything other than eating).


much-1, degree-2

It means "more" or "a lot." It is more (in terms of standard) used and generally is polite/formal too; e.g., nampa ga ōii. In its non-modifier form, it is yōi (yooi), but you won't often see this. "Yōi ga ōii" (lit. "more is more") or "yōi ga ii" (a lot is good). Rule: yooi (non-modifier) and ooi (modifier).

kiss-1, thing-2

hide-1, conceal-1

Fachipa, in its most basic sense, means to hide. It's semantic space is deeper than that, obviously. For example: fachipamasu refers to someone that conceals. In the pejorative sense, you say "fachipamasuta," which is an incredibly rude term. In the metaphorical sense, it describes people who conceal something by "blending in with the dark."


inside-1

zō means "inside." It is used over chika, -re, etc., sometimes. For example, you'd usually say: yowa no zō ji zhupi uenae (there's a spider in the container). But this isn't necessary, as you can say: "yowa ji zhupi uenae," and be understood, without making redundant sentences. Generally, zō is more elevated, formal, or has different use cases, but is preferable in poetry, literature, and some specific constructions.


hot air balloon-2

Kiebakaden (lit. "air circle car") refers to a hot air balloon. If you meant a simple balloon, then you might say kiebaka ("air circle").


extreme-2

Mabui means 'extreme / violent.' It is often used as a modifier (mabuuii) to mark something as 'state of the art / cream of the crap.' Example: "mabuuii e" (perfectly okay / extremely okay - basically a way to say "the most sound/OK ever"). Another example: "mabuuii ii" (the bestest ever). Literally, though, these would be translated as "cuttingly..." Note that mabuu is the casual verbal form; you'd say "mabuiruii" formally. You cannot use "mabuiinu" because that would become an adverb and imply what's modified is a verb, unless marked explicitly as a noun. So: "mabuiinu ii" (to make the best / to extremely better). It is also used in forms like: "mabuuii kana," literally meaning "the most of words," but, semantically, it refers to words that cuts through and reaches the deep heart (nukore) of someone. It can be an adverb too: mabuiinu fofu (to cuttingly speak; i.e., speak so loud that the sound actually cuts through your ears. It describes horrible screaming, screeching, and tantrums of children that give you tinnitus.) As a verb on its own, it can mean to pierce/cut, though it implies piercing. For example, you don't say "ba ou mabuui) - "to pierce a sweet fruit." You generally say: "ba ou faja / ba ou himae" (to split a sweet fruit OR to mutilate into convenient servings a sweet fruit). But you do say "hon ou mabuui" (to pierce a man). Note that mabui is usually an obligate modifier. This means that it HATES to function as a verb in the examples I showed. You'd say: "hon o mabuii ue" (the person is pierced (by someone)) or "suno momg hon o mabuii ue" (I really pierced a person / my words cut through him). If this was in a good sense (i.e., you made him see something), keep as is or use explicit mabuioii. If negative (heartbreak / hurting someone badly), use mabuitai. Usually positive/meliorative by default (mabui).

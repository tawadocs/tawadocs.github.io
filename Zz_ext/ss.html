unpa e mi, musi e sijelo mi

borrowed directly from Gyakyyi, which loaned it from older Takan languages. The word's origin was in Kawakanamagana, which loaned it from Greek, in which it meant to masturbate. Maraca in Modern Tawa simply refers to any act of pleasure or pleasing something or oneself. It could be physical, sensory pleasure, such as warm baths, or it could be emotional pleasure, derived through touch or song. When made romantic, as in maracanyu, it carries a sexual connotation. Since it is not mandatory for maraca to carry -(r)u or -nyu, often times, the context disambiguates if maraca is being used romantically or platonically. Explicitly marking it romantic, though, as in maracanyu / maracat, makes it sexual too. When sexual, it is a pretty vulgar word, referring to sexual stimulation, masturbation, or fondling one's genitals (touching oneself). It can refer to orgasms too (~maracanyata) or ecstatic feelings (~maracata). In the lower forms, such as maracasta, it can refer to self-care or doing something to feel better. Like, 'Kamteikikitãsun ni o maracastanai' (~my favourite food soothed me a little). Maracat would be soothing with an ulterior motive and maracaoy would be soothing positively - esp. in the context of people-pleasing and as such. Do note that maracat can also be used for sexual pleasure, as in "Kim Jong Uun ni maracanaitmas no komuni" (Kim Jong Un is master of goon). (maracanait = masturbate, preferred form (as opposed to maracatnai)).

pakala e telo, kama tan poki

it refers to anything from emotional slips (when you can't help but smile and it shows, as it is genuine, even though you don't want it to) to literal liquid spills, like in the idiomatic expression 'chisuno taranai' (my water has been spilled). It can imply agency in chisuna taranai (my water has been spilled (due to me)) or when paired with 'ni,' the focus could be on the state (of being spilled) itself. Tara can also refer to leaking something private, such as secrets, or flaws in boundaries. This 'flaw' can be literal (a bottle with a little hole) or more abstract (a relationship without proper privacy). Note: tara is not in the core lexicon because this can technically be boiled down to a core word.


mama telo

bopu is a word used to describe anything short of a breast. as a verb, it can refer to lactation, and as a noun, it refers to any milk-producing organ in a living thing, including udders and tits. It's not inherently a vulgar word but it can be if used casually or randomly. It also describes bouncy, breast-like, or saggy things (daminq bopu) depending on context, though this essentially like saying 'that looks like a saggy breast' - probably not something you'd use in normal daily conversation, unless you want to be called a shifumo.


sewi telo

Sorama is a newer term, as opposed to Sorachi. Soramaru would be to rain, whereas formally or poetically it is 'sorala chiru' (the sky waters). These are the other -ma weathers: reretema (hail), retema (cold weather), nashitama (heatwaves, hot weather), kiukoma (windy, turbulent), bakama (tornado), chimama (tsunami), sowma (sunny), meiyama (cloudy), reteshima (snowy), damima (mild, clear, temperate), kamama (low visibility, dark, foggy), mamama (stormy), tahetema (thunderstorm), pokichima (typhoon/hurricane), rereteshima (blizzard), sosowma (drought), reretemama (severe, intense, big hail). Do note that each can further derive even more roots with derivational particles: tahetemaoy (nice thunderstorm), tahetemat (unpleasant thunderstorm), mamamata (happy storm / a storm of happy emotion), etc. And, of course, you would use soramangu for "weather" itself.


toki, toki ante

Gana refers to language. Ganairime would be linguistics, Ganaumashí could be sociolinguistics, Ganashí would be a phoneme. Ganangutsu is... well, a mystery. So depending on context, ganangutsu would refer to a specific theory of the root of language. Ganasumatà would be the essence of language - a unique human method of communication via thoughts and symbols. And so on. Gana comes from the Kawakanamagana word for 'language.' In many contexts, gana can also refer to grammar (like gana ribu - grammar book), though you can use 'ganatachi' or ganatachitei for grammar.


toki ante

loaned from the Takan languages, agona refers to a dialect. It is a different way of speaking. Agonanya would be a gynolect (female genderlect) and agonanye would be androlect (male genderlect). The derivation is similar, so you can derive many technical concepts with this.


sewi, jan sewi

General filler word. It doesn't refer to God Himself but, rather, any deity that is like God. It's a tame, mild word. Zhamu is also used in poetic constructions, such as 'janà no zhamunya' (goddess from heaven (romantic)). It's also used in the formal greeting 'are zhamua barekunai zuõ' (may God bless you, where 'are' marks the wish / hope (may - optative)). The word 'gopo' is rarely used as a replacement by speakers that have trouble pronouncing zhamu.


seme

either hya or hyo can be paired with -ngu to mean interrogative. When turned into a verb, like hyungu, then it means to question.

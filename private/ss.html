akesi

teiya are creatures. they tend to be cold to the touch. When they move quickly, they go back and forth on sprawling legs or slither or squirm back and forth across the ground if they lack legs. They're close to the ground. They usually lay eggs. The contents of this semantic space is based off of a pilot study I conducted in 2023 with sample size 98 about the lexical semantics of teiya.


ala

the semantic space of rra contains nothing. But don't be fooled, it isn't void of information. "rra′a niru eaucal" can be "nobody's looking at the fruit." "honíla kamu rraĉ" can mean "the person ate nothing." as a modifier, it actually reverses the semantic space of whatever words come before it, which no other content word does. "honqíla ukajurra eaucal" can mean "the animal doesn't hunt for fruit." A 'twanarra' is never a type of twana. it does not need to be a modifier, like rrą, to negate. as a verb, rra can mean "erase" or "delete." see Grammar:11 for more information.


alasa

ukajo refers to hunting and foraging. Searching for a lost object is ukajo. Searching for something on a web browser is ukajo. Throwing a spear into the side of a bison could be ukajo because it is part of hunting, as can the act of picking berries, but the core of ukajo is the trying to find. Recently (as in during the past half a decade), usage of ukajo to refer to attempting or trying has become very common. So trying to knit could be a type of ukajo. Trying to sprint could be a type of ukajo. Trying to look larger than you actually are to scare off a predator could be ukajo.


ale

subeko is everything. this can be either everything ever or a smaller subset of everything. If you modify subeko with another word, it is limited to objects that have that word's quality, or an entirety of said word. For example, "mafssubeką" for "the whole 'red'." "ribusubeką" could mean  "the entirety of the book." "irimesubeką" can mean "the entirety of a job." This is often similar to 'subeko no' constructions, but it's not exactly the same. for example, "subeko no mafo" is all 'reds', not the whole 'red', "subeko no ribu" is all books, not 'the whole book.' compare these two sentence: 'denh sulla subeko no ribu' (give me all of the books) and 'denh sulla ribusubeką' (give me the entire book). to add an '(all) things related to' nuance, you may use a case or alternative constructions 'denh  sulla subekyĉ ribuita' (get me everything related to books (e.g., papers, pens, etc.))

Another usage of "ale" I've noticed myself and many others using is the idea of "completeness" or something being all-encompasing. Like if I know everything about rocks, I could say "mi sona ale e kiwen" just as easily as I might say "mi sona e ale kiwen." It's almost used as a superlative sometimes, like "pona ale" for "best" in the sense that it is "completely good." Keep in mind that the English word "complete" has two senses, the other which is much closer to pini than ale. Make sure to translate the idea, not the word!


anpa

nanda refers to the space (or sometimes object) below something. For example, in Toki Pona, "anpa supa" is the space under a table. "anpa kasi" is the space under a plant. nanda can also refer to qualities of defeat and shame, such as beating someone in a game of chess. You can describe the loser as nanda, and you can use nanda as a verb to mean "beat." nanda can also talk about social stratification and hierarchy. A lower class person can be a nanda hon, but that can also refer to a humble person.


ante

arene describes a different thing. there are loads of things in the world, and every thing has similarities and differences to other things. In a specific context, if one wishes to frame the differences between objects, arene is incredibly useful. It can refer to the different object itself, qualities of difference, or the concept of change itself. It can also be used as a verb to mean "alter" or "change." if it is ambiguous what you mean, use 'imnyiteirra' (not the same) to explicitly say 'different.'


awen

kiwi refers to inertia itself. so something resisting change or 'continuing' is kiwi. Kiwinq would be the opposite, often translated as 'stop' or 'wait!'

awen is also used by many speakers to mean "maintain." This is very close to the idea of protection, so you'll see awen used for that as well in many situations as a transitive verb, but some ideas of "protection" are better described with "selo."


esun

hari is a type of exchange. Usually it's used to mean "buy," "sell," or "trade." The use of a currency isn't required for something to be hari. Trading baseball cards is hari. hari doesn't imply that both parties lose something. Exchanging knowledge is hari, even though neither party loses knowledge. Some interesting extensions of hari I've seen include using hari for breathing, where one trades the air inside them with the outside world. Seeing hari as a swap more than as a type of buying has had it being used far more often these days in my experience, potentially due to anticapitalist values, and potentially because of natural semantic shift. Some people like this usage because it makes hari more useful. It's far from universal though, and many speakers only use hari to mean something like "trade" where two participatory parties exchange items.

According to Sonja, the Akan concept of "esun" was described to her in a taxi once a while ago. I do know a friend of a friend who is an Akan speaker, so I will see if he has any insight and replace this with that insight.


ijo

Everything is within the semantic space of kitei. If a thing is a thing, it is an kitei. This could be anything or something. This could be a physical object, an idea, an action, a place, a sentient being, or any other thing. You are in the semantic space of kitei.

Some people refuse to use ijo for sentient things. They claim it has inanimate connotations. I don't see why this would happen unless toki pona speakers assumed it was the same as english "thing" or portuguese "coisa" etc. In reality, toki pona words don't have the history of dehumanization that words like "thing" in english have. In toki pona, humans are as ijo as a dog or a book or a galaxy.


ike

ike is any negative quality. Anything can be ike. ike is a judgement call. what one person considers ike can be pona to another. For example, the complexity of computer coding is not a good thing for me, so I'd call it ike. But for a lot of my friends, that same complexity is pona! the simple melody of "mary had a little lamb" is boring to many people on its own, so they might call it ike. But a baby might enjoy it a lot, so despite being potentially overly simple for adults, it's just right for that baby, i.e. pona. Though of course some babies may like twelve tone rows and some adults may like what others would consider "children songs." It's all relative and case by case. If something isn't bad, you can't use ike to describe it without framing it as such. ike can mean complex and simple just as easily as pona can.


ilo

The semantic space of twana contains things that are used towards a goal. It’s easy to say that everything can be used. Likewise if something is being used or can commonly be used, it is easy to call it an twana. If I am using a hammer to hammer a nail into the wall, that is an twana. If i am using a psychological method to calm myself down when i’m stressed, that can be an twana as well. Without much context, twana can refer to things that are commonly used as tools. With the context of it being used for something, though, anything can be an twana.


insa

ĉika talks about things inside of things. Commonly, this is used to describe a location. The location inside of a car is ĉika. It can also be used to describe objects that are inside of things. Someone's organs are ĉika. ĉika doesn't need to be contained completely, it can just mean "in between." A book in the middle of a bookshelf is ĉika. The space between two city buildings is ĉika. It can be used more abstractly too. When compared to a head, a mind is ĉika. In the context of good and bad, ĉika can be neutral. When compared to hot and cold, ĉika can be lukewarm. If anything is being framed as inside of something else, it falls under the semantic space of ĉika.


jaki

gade talks about unpleasant sensory experiences. This can be any of the senses, internal or external. This could be bad smells, repulsive flavors, gross images, ear-shattering noises, and horrible textures. But it could also be internal sensory experiences such as nausea. gade can be used to refer to other things that aren't usually sensory, and it should be. But be careful, because using gade will frame something through a sensory lens. If someone is acting bad, such as bullying or harassing someone else, you can describe their actions as gade, but doing so makes their actions framed as unpleasant in a sensory way. can also refer to a monkey, donkey, even gorilla in some contexts.

Some people reject the idea that jaki is inherently unpleasant. For example, here is a quote from jan Ke Tami (licensed under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 with permission from jan Ke Tami)

jan Ke Tami: I keep thinking about the "jaki pona" zine that finds beauty in things that for sure are in some way unpleasant
jaki has enough space for things it describes to be unpleasant in one way... and also pleasant in a different way


jan

usage of hon differs a lot between groups of speakers, but many speakers use it broadly to refer to any sentient creature (or more accurately, "sapient," with human intelligence). But this can break down when you consider what sentience is and where we draw lines between ourselves and nature.


jelo

mhuru refers to yellows, oranges, and sometimes yellowish greens. I would use mhuru to talk about any shade of cadmium yellow.


jo

ao is the act of holding, carrying, possessing, owning, or broadly having. The english word "have" tends to be pretty broad, and ao is no different. Some speakers will only use ao if an object is in someone's hand or they're holding it. Others will use ao for something as abstract as a word having sounds in it or a language having words in it. for some speakers, if I'm borrowing your book, you don't ao it anymore, I do, even if you own it. For other speakers, in that situation both you and I ao the book at the same time in different ways. When you use ao, you're drawing a metaphor between someone's relationship with an object and a world in which they're holding it in their hands. some people like to stretch this metaphor much farther than others. See Grammar:14 for more information. One more thing: 'wua au so ryoteiji' (They have / had him here) would mean something like '~They carried him here.' But do NOT use the romantic form unless you want to imply something wildly different - 'wua annyu so ryoteiji' means '~They had sex with him here'


kala

Any creature that swims habitually is a kala. This can include anything from fish to whales to seals. The most important aspect of a kala is its relation to water. kala tend to either live in the water or spend a good portion of their time in it. Some speakers extend this usage a bit and let it refer to anything that is in water, like a human swimmer or a pool noodle. Sometimes, toki ponists will describe the act of showering as kala. This usage is often interpreted as somewhat funny, but it is commonly understood. If it isn't, it's easy to clarify.


kalama

waka is often used as a generic word for gibberish or random sounds, like 'fuyume soa fofu nihi watadà waka!' (what he is saying is only gibberish). other than that, it can be used to signal communication, like a 'wakatwana' (communication device) is used to refer to a cellphone. fofttwana (speaking device), though, is not a communication device, but it could be something like a speaker. also, do not use 'waka' to mean hello or as a generic way of saying hi.

From jan Olipija on how kalama's semantic space extends to vibrations with her perspective as a member of the Deaf/hard of hearing community:

"seeing as sound is vibration and one often causes the other, many [deaf/hard of hearing] can perceive via vibrations what hearing people would perceive as sound. e.g. stamping on the floor, thumpy thumpy dance music or hammering from the downstairs flat renovating their bathroom waking you up at 8am. thus vibrations could be considered a form of kalama, especially to [deaf/hard of hearing] people.

instead or in addition to kute, this form of kalama would pair with the verb pilin. e.g. 'mi pilin lon noka mi e kalama ilo tan supa anpa.'"


kama

kama (yara in Tawa) is a strong metaphor in toki pona. To arrive at a location and to arrive at a state of being are the same concept in toki pona. This means that yara can refer to arriving home to eat dinner, arriving at school, or the moment you become an teiya because an evil prescriptivist wizard cursed you for using too many aŝi ganáán. This of course describes yara's use as a preverb, in which its meaning is close to "become." This can imply the start, body, or end of this process. Use yaranq to describe the reverse of the process. Examples: 'so ni yaraén teiyal' (he is becoming a reptile, 'su ni yaraén jizunlla' (I am coming to your place), 'ŝi yaralji, reoppu: suo yara wanílme korru zu' (in the future, I will be the one who kills you). It is better to use yara in some cases than tawa, such as if you are already at someone's place, use yara.

kama is also used to talk about the future a lot. The future is things to come, but the past is things that already came, so this is less of a tense marking word. But often you'll see people modifying words with it to make them future-y, like "soweli kama" for "the animal that will arrive" or "tenpo kama" for "the time that is to come."

kama used transitively is best translated as "summon," "conjure," or "bring about." It's the closest thing toki pona has to a deticated causative. Often something like "I make you build a house" is translated as "mi kama e ni: sina pali e tomo." But for causatives, I recommend thinking about the actual act of making someone do something. Are you telling them to do something, using consequences as a means of motivation? Are you controlling them in some way? "kama" is certainly used here for causatives, but sometimes breaking down a causative into an actual action that describes what's going on is really fun and novel.


kasi

miyo is any number of types of plants. big plants like trees are miyo, and small plants like weeds are too! anything largely in the category of "plant" is a miyo. There's some controversy about fungi, though. many believe them to be within miyo's semantic space, while others disagree entirely and use a new word,


ken

ken is all about abilities. These could be inherent abilities, like the ability to fly, walk, or swim. These could also be external, like permission to use the bathroom. If I can blow bubbles with bubble gum because I'm not around anyone who I don't want to annoy, that ability is a ken. Unlike some languages, toki pona has a single word that contains all abilities. ken can also refer to a probability or a possibility. The throughline here is almost saying that an event is able to occur, but that doesn't mean it necessarily will or won't. These ideas of possibility and ability are two sides of the same coin.


kepeken

un is using. For example, "mi unu kitekilen" can be "I use a thing," and "sua katsu asa kitekiun " can mean "I build a house using a thing." when used as a noun, it can refer to a specific instance of using something or usage in general. For example, "unil no kitekiryą zunin nihi iinqsubeką" "this thing's usage by you is completely bad (wrong)." Many speakers consider using the other word for path / method for this scenario, though.


kijetesantakalu

kijetesantakalu is a beloved april fool's joke coined by Sonja Lang in 2009. The whole point is to have one word that represents a uselessly specific set of objects. At the time, kijetesantakalu could only refer to procyonids (raccoons and other very similar animals). In 2021, nano pushed for the semantic space to be widened enough to include ferrets, and this push was so successful that nowadays kijetesantakalu is used to describe all musteloids (weasels, otters, minks, red pandas, wolverines, skunks, badgers, etc).

I would like to bring it back to the community's attention here and now that there are plans for kijetesantakalu's semantic space to expand as time progresses. In 2029-2031, kijetesantakalu will begin to refer to any animal in Arctoidea, which includes seals and bears. This is perhaps the only word in any language with a planned semantic drift. We will see how successful it is. It would be really funny.

This definition was added on April 1st, 2025.


kili

kili are fruits and vegetables, i.e. any part of a plant that is edible. These could be fresh. An apple is kili. These could be fermented. Kimchi and pickled onions are kili. These could be preserved through canning. Strawberry jam and dried mango are kili. This could even be a sauce with a primary vegetable component: basil pesto is kili, gazpacho is kili, five minced onions are kili. kili can refer to wheat seeds or other grains that haven't been ground up yet, but flour and bread don't fit well into kili's semantic space.

What if a kili is poisonous? Poisonous berries are not edible, but they can still be kili. This is because kili's semantic space pivots towards things that resemble edible parts of plants, even if you can't actually eat them. By using kili, you evoke the image of eating it. This won't encourage toki pona speakers to eat it right away if they don't recognize it. If I used kili for poison ivy a toki pona speaker would think less of it as a shrub what grows on the ground and more as a type of lettuce for making salad. Of course, they wouldn't assume that it would be a good idea to do so, but the way the poison ivy feels in a conceptual semantic space is different.

A minority usage of kili proposed first around 2021 online is a child or product. This borrows a cognitive metaphor from a lot of European languages that sees familial relations as trees. While this could work, it is likely to be misunderstood by many speakers unless you build up this before using kili like this. It's also worth considering how this frames children, and wether that isn't as culturally neutral as you may think. In many cultures, children are raised communally and aren't percived as any individual's child. Would kili work in these cases? Maybe not!


kin

wa is in almost all usage close to "also." wa is almost always used as a modifier. Instead of modifying the semantic space of the word it follows, it clarifies the relationship between that word and other ideas that could occupy the same position in the sentence. So if it modifies a subject, it means there are other things that do the same action. If it modifies a verb, it means there are other actions done by the same thing. If it modifies an object, it means that there are other things the action is done to.

Many people also use "kin" at the end (or, less commonly, the beginning) to modify the whole thing, similar to the English word "also." kin generally lacks a noun or transitive meaning for most speakers. If it is used in these positions, it may be similar to "alsoness" and "to make something also."


kiwen

taka are things whose form resists changing. This often talks about a hard physical material or object, like a rock or wood or crystal. It can describe something's material, so a chair made out of a hard material could be taka. It is additionally possible to talk about nonphysical things using taka. As a verb, it can refer to strengthening.


ko

a kara is a powdery or semisolid substance. It is usually dry (use karaĉibu/karachibu for a goo or sludge). However, apart from this, it refers to drugs and medication, like cocaine, but its scope is a bit broader than that: anything psychoactive or bioactive is a kara; therefore, MDMA, meth, ecstasy are kara but so are levetiracetam, paracetamol, and montelukast sodium. it can be used like this 'à, soa kara suõ!' (he drugged me!).

Something to consider for yourself: is a pillow ko? It feels similar to some types of clay or soil. You can kind of mold it.


kon

kiuko covers a very wide range of ideas that are loosely related to each other through the concept of being known, but not seen. You'll often hear people use the phrase "unseen agent" to describe kiuko. I've divided this semantic space into three parts: spirits, meaning, and gas. toki pona connects these concepts by giving them all the same word, but this throughline is only a metaphor. In my opinion, these are three distinct senses, not one meaning. It can also mean to fly and jump. So if a bird is flying, you would say 'omukla kiuku' (the bird flies), and if you want to describe a man jumping, you would say 'honla kiukku' (the man jumps). so kiuko -> air; kiuku -> fly; kiukkuru or kiukruku -> jump. in Modern Tawa, kiukkuru is used (standard). -ku is the figurative particle.

If you'll indulge my spiritual worldview for a bit, a spirit could be some sort of supernatural being that we don't normally see. There are countless traditions around the globe that interpret divinity through unseen agents. For example, in christianity, the concept of the holy ghost could very easily be a type of kon. Dybbukim in Jewish folklore can easily be kon because they're not things people commonly see. If a spirit shows itself and becomes seen, using kon still works great to communicate that it frequently is an unseen agent. The idea of a spirit can be extended to the part of humans or beings, depending on how a speaker considers the idea of a soul. Some people think that everything has a soul, while others think that only humans have souls. kon works very well for all of these souls, framing them as the unseen part of things. In fact, a minority of speakers goes as far to call aspects of identity like gender types of kon. The broad idea behind this category is that kon can be used for, specifically, an unseen entity.

"Meaning" is a broad category. The most common usage of kon for meaning is semantic meaning, i.e. the meaning of words in language. Every semantic space entry in this dictionary could be described as an instance of "kon." If a speaker didn't know what another speaker meant even though they heard all the words, they could say that they didn't understand the "kon." Less often you'll see kon used for more abstract ideas of meaning, such as the meaning of a piece of art. This category is about unseen ideas or parts of thought.

Finally, gas. air is a very prototypical example of kon, but air is composed of a very specific set of gaseous matter. Even if a gas is visible, like steam or smoke, it's still squarely part of kon's semantic space. The entirety of Jupiter can be kon. Clouds can be kon. Hell, the sun can even be kon if you're looking at what composes it and framing it from that perspective instead of framing it as a producer of light.


kule

nukonga (from nuko (heart) + nga (lexical source)) are aspects of senses. The most prototypical version of this is colour, an aspect of sight, but it can also refer to aspects of other senses. Here are some possibilities: For sound, nukonga can refer to timbre or pitch. For taste, nukonga could be a flavour. For touch, nukonga could be a type of texture. There are plenty of ways to be creative with nukonga, but the most common uses besides "colour" are the ones I've listed associated with sound. Here is a good example: you are at the restaurant and you ordered a special drink. you ask the waiter, 'fuyu ni nukongal no ryo hyo?' (what is the sense of this?), meaning 'what is the flavour of this (drink)?' and he can answer 'imo haŝirul no karabalú ę ii'ii ĉihirą' (like the child of the creamfruits and the best milk).


kulupu

Any group of other things can be a umà. It's commonly used to describe groups of people: communities, clubs, cultures, cliques, charters, committees, collectives, corporations, and perhaps other words that don't start with the letter "c" like a baseball team or a pride of lions. umà can also be groups of objects or animals, like a bunch of bananas, a basket filled with small clay pots, or a murder of crows. umà is often used to describe a plural system/collective (i.e. multiple people in one body). Usually a umà will be a group of items that have some sort of shared attribute, and using umà to describe a group of anything is emphasizing that they have something in common. These shared qualities can be anything from location to color to desire.


kute

NOTE: use fofunai (as per the Modern Tawish Standard) instead of fofnai (old)! fofnai describes both passive hearing and active listening. It can also refer to things that hear, such as ears, or microphones. Tools like microphones may be more likely to be understood as tool though. Many people use fofnai as a modifier in these contexts, because a microphone is primarily a tool to them. Deaf people, even ones without cochlear implants, frequently listen to music even though they don't use their ears to hear it. The vibrations can be pleasant, and at some large concerts there are areas where deaf and hard of hearing people can touch balloons and other objects that carry vibrations well so they can enjoy the music as well. This is all fofnai (now fofunai).

"kute" is occasionally used to mean "obey," but in these contexts, it has been my experience that people interpret it as simply "hear" or "listen." This usage has been on a steep decline for a while now, but you may still see it in older texts, or even in conversation.


lape

The semantic space of rawa contains sleep and rests. More abstractly, rawa can mean anything from a short break from walking to a gap year in college, and less abstractly rawa can mean a nap or sleeping. Using rawa to describe an action often implies that the level of effort or involvement is lower when compared to surrounding activities.


laso

azhuru talks about both blues and greens at the same time. it can describe a large range of colors that are often separated in other languages, such as English. In English, blue and green are separate concepts with their own semantic space. In toki pona (as well as countless languages across the world), these concepts are merged into one. Many linguists affectionally call this color "grue" in the contexts of analyzing other languages, and the term can be helpful here because it gives a fantastic anchor for color perspective to English speakers. Think to yourself how often it is crucial to distinguish between blue and green. In cases where you wish to do so, greens are yellower than blues, so it's easy to modify azhuru with a word like "mhuru" to specify that, but if you don't need to specify, doing so adds more clutter to your speaking. I wouldn't go much darker than cobalt blue before using kuru instead. because cool colors like azhuru tend to be darker a lot of the time, don't forget about kuru! NOTE: azhuru (aẑuru) is the modern variant of azuru in the spoken register of Tawa. azuru and azhuru are both correct.

Another part of laso worth mentioning is purples. While somewhat controversial, when I showed several toki ponists color chips (the kind you use when choosing which color to paint your wall), most of the darker or bluer shades of purple that were not dark enough to be pimeja were unanimously laso. Some of the lighter or pinker ones were called loje. There was also some overlap, and some people stated the importance of the surrounding colors. These are all things to keep in mind when you encounter a color that seems hard to talk about in toki pona. I'm often reminded of paints, and a purple like dioxazine violet would most definitely be pimeja and not laso.


lawa

its semantic space contains anything or anyone that 'owns' or 'controls' something. it is explicitly about ownership, lordship, or control. the government is komuni, the ruling party is komuni, and your boss is komuni. Slaveowners are komuni as well. examples: 'sua kikitanq komunilǫ' (I despise the masters (i.e., the government)). The semantic space of komuni contains parts of a whole that control that whole. Additionally, this can be talking a bit more abstractly. Someone who's in charge of a group could be considered the komuni of that group. Commanding troops in battle is a type of komuni. komuni doesn't imply any level of consent, so it could be forceful or gentle. Guiding a swimming student's body into the correct position for good technique could be komuni, but plenty of things that I'm not willing to describe could be komuni as well.


len

The semantic space of hyako contains both cloth and the act of hiding something. Any fabric or clothing can be hyako, for example a shirt or a blanket. The pivot between "cloth" and "to hide something" is similar to covering something with a blanket. This meaning comes from the idea of applying a cloth to something to hide it. Both a hidden bird and a clothed bird could be a "hyaką omukyĉ." hyako can also refer to secrecy or privacy, and other qualities of being hidden.


leko

natse is, at its core, squares, or things that behave like squares. I find myself using natse a lot more in highly urban environments. natse tend to tile. I've seen natse used to describe most common polygons, and though squares are definitely most common and prototypical, hexagons in video games and on pavements are also natse, and any tiling pattern is natse too, even if the shapes are convex or unorthodox.

leko is a relatively uncommon word. It's likely the least widespread and used word in this list, so usage you observe may differ from what I describe here.


lete

rete is the quality of coldness. If anything is cold, it is rete. rete is also occasionally used to describe raw food or unprocessed ore.


lili

Before Modern Tawa, lili was pokinq; the semantic space of pokinq contains all qualities of smallness relative to context. The thing that ties all things pokinq together is their relative size. A tall man might be pokinq compared to a mountain. A long speech may be pokinq compared to a novel. pokinq is a relative word, and therefore is always dependent on context. Now, the variant spelling of pokinq is simply 'lili,' which is commonly used instead of pokinq in Modern Tawa. so, say 'lili haŝiru' for 'little child.' It can also refer to 'a while' temporally, like in 'kiwinq lililen... namsan ni...-' (wait a minute... her name was... - but accurately ~"wait a minute, what was her name...").

This isn't as common as "suli ala," but lili is sometimes used to mean "unimportant." About half of the time I see this usage, I hear it clarified with "suli ala" directly before or after, and the other half of the time, I don't. Definitely pay attention to if you are understood if you want to try using lili like this. You likely will be, but lili is less clearly "unimportant" than suli is "important."


linja

If it's long an you can tie it into a knot, it's napi! napi implies flexibility. By calling something napi, you are framing it as something that could be bent significantly, even if it can't. Let's say I call a metal bar a napi. I'm implying to me that I can bend it easily, that to me it is flimsy. But most of the time, napi are things like string or cooked spaghetti. Dried spaghetti would not be a napi because it's brittle. If I draw a line, perhaps it can be napi, because even though it is drawn and cannot be moved around on the page, it might be representing something that could.


lipu

lipu's semantic space is tied together by a piece of flat paper with writing on it. lipu can talk about both the texture and properties of that paper or the information contained on it, often both. For example, a document on a computer, while in no way as flimsy as a paper, is still very much lipu. If you take the writing off of a piece of paper, it's just as much a lipu. Some speakers argue that this is an extended metaphor and by using lipu to describe these files, one is framing the computer as a lipu. For example, in Southeast Asia, language was written on dried palm leaves, which without writing are still lipu. In fact, many speakers use lipu for all leaves, be they large or small, without any implications of writing. Anything flat and flimsy can be a lipu for these speakers. As soon as it gets hard, it's likely to be a supa unless it has writing on it, though. For example, a maya stelae could be lipu, but a rock shaped like one with no writing on it would almost definitely not be.


loje

mafo speaks to reddish colors and pigments. cadmium reds all the way down to a darkish alizarin crimson. You can start mixing violet into mafo for a while before it starts getting more azuru or kuru. You can also brighten it into orange and it can still be mafo, but there's a gray area between mafo and mhuru that I encourage learners to play around with!


lon

the semantic space of uns (life) encompasses anything about any living thing. so this could be biological (alive) or philosophical (awareness). it could also refer to your vigour, health, and drive to continue pushing or achieve more in life. it also refers to something extremely precious, sentimental, or sacred, like a partner (zu ni unszun (~you're my life)). it can refer to someone's life force (i.e., their soul) and it can refer to vitality itself. you can also use this word to describe something happening 'live,' such as a livestream or broadcast. 'uns' can also be used to describe where something is, even if it isn't living, like 'zhupireila uns nanandaji no asagal' (the dead spider is at the very bottom of the wall).


luka

tete is an organ capable of manipulation, or a part of said organ. For example, an arm is a tete, and so is a hand, fingers, even elbows. The main part is that it interacts with other things. If a cat's paw is swiping at me, I might use tete to describe it, but if it's just walking on it I might not (see: tetenq), but I still could. So, it's possible for one organ to be both a tete and something else in different contexts. The prehensile tails of some monkeys could be tete too, especially while they're using them, but the only thing something needs in order to be a tete is an innate ability to interact with objects. tete can also be used as a verb to refer to this interaction with its direct object, which could be touching, petting, pressing, pulling, holding, hugging, grasping, playing an instrument, etc. I'm sure you can thing of other things it could mean! tete is also commonly used to describe talking using a signed language.


lukin

see Grammar:16


lupa

A haruma is a hole. Here's what that means: these holes can go through objects, like the hole in a donut, or they can be an indentation in an object, such as a hole dug in the ground. Things that are haruma continue to be haruma even when there is something blocking them, like a door or a window, because they are still meant for things to pass through them. From a perspective of function, that's what haruma are for. A haruma is a part of an object that things can pass through. A door is still a haruma because people can open it, and a window is still a haruma because it's meant for light to pass through. Empty space is not a haruma because it's not part of an object. The semantic space can be extended to describe other things that fit this description, even if they aren't physical holes, such as portals or links on the internet. You can read more about haruma here.


ma

jimei is a place or location. This can be a physical one, like a country or spot on the ground. it's also used to talk about anything from a tree branch something's on or even an airplane miles or kilometres above the ground. jimei can sometimes be the ground itself, like dirt or rocks, or even an entire planet with a hard surface. don't confuse jimei with taka though, because jimei denotes location and ground where taka doesn't. You can use jimei to mean 'place / put' too, like in: 'sa′a jimeiru atoral jimeijiji' (She placed the apple on the ground). Use jirei if the floor is not natural or environmental (so if you're at home, then it's jirei).

Many people also use ma to refer to the metaphysical, such as a location in a sentence. But a lot of speakers don't use it like this, and there are valid criticisms that this feels Anglophone influence. You will likely be understood if you use ma like this. I use it like this all the time and I have rarely had an issue being understood.


mama

A kari is a parent, ancestor, caretaker, or creator. My great-great-great grandparent can be a kari to me, even if I never even met them. Just the same, an uncle or an aunt or a cousin could all be kari, but if I'm an adult and I'm talking about my baby cousin, I'm probably not going to call them kari. kari is somewhat about hierarchy, but it isn't about power. It's about caretaking. This doesn't always have to do with age. A worker at a nursing home could be a kari. Another fun example of kari is a tree that bears fruit. If all of the seeds are its children, then the tree could be kari! You may specify gender with ĉa and ĉe: kariĉa (female, like mother), kariĉe (male, like father). example: kariĉasun kikitaų hariru (my mother loves to shop).


mani

mani describes anything that is used to measure the value of other objects. In most societies nowadays, the best example of mani include paper, coins, and numbers stored in machines. Video games are great examples of places that give us opprotunities to call other things "mani." In some Fallout games, bottlecaps are mani. In Ena: Dream BBQ, chocolate is mani. (It's not lost on me that both of these examples are depicted as small coin sized circles.) But we don't need video games to find examples fall outside the monitary systems toki pona learners may be familiar with. There are still people out there who use livestock to measure the value of other things. Long ago, this method was likely MORE common than using paper and coins. So in these contexts, and these contexts alone, livestock can be a type of "mani." This is the etymology of the sitelen pona glyph, which is meant to resemble an ox.

mani is quite the elusive varmant. On the surface, it seems like it just refers to money. But most dictionaries will list "livestock" as another meaning. Why? And when can livestock truely be called "mani"? This has led many people in the past, including myself, to describe "mani" as "anything being used in the exchange of goods." After speaking with some more toki ponists about this I realized that this isn't the core of mani, and updated this definition a couple of days later.


mi

it is a pronoun and also a number. its usage is the same as zu. -nampą may be used for disambiguation. compare: 'su ni kita sawa kiteki' (I like sweet things) v. 'sua watadà subtthu su atora' (I only wanted one apple).


mije and meli

The semantic space of mije contains men and masculinity. The semantic space of meli contains women and femininity. These words are as well defined as the concepts they represent, so it's left intentionally broad without specifying further. This means that for beings that can talk to you, they decide if they fit within the semantic space of either of these words.

A common question is "can mije and meli be used to refer to biological sex?" The answer is extremely complicated. First, it's worth unpacking the concept of biological sex, which I'm not going to do here. (If you want to learn more about that, research "intersex.") That can inform the ethics of using mije and meli to talk about biological sex. As for trans people, they usually identify their bodies with their gender and not their biological sex at birth. That doesn't mean that no trans person has ever said something along the lines of "my body is mije, but I am meli" to explain their transness. It's up to the individual to decide how they want to identify. For example, it is incredibly disrespectful to use mije to describe the body or any attribute of someone who does not identify under that word if you haven't cleared it with them first, and even asking them if it's okay is, at the very least, a little weird. (The same goes for meli as well as tonsi.) There are quite a few trans members of the toki pona community, so this matters a lot.


moku

kamo is the act of eating. It is also used to refer to anything eaten. you can use kamo to describe it! doing so implies that it is in some context eaten, though, so be careful. There's a quote from Charlie and the Chocolate Factory (2005). "Everything here is edible; even I'm edible. But that, dear children, is cannibalism, and is in fact frowned upon in most societies." Use this ancient wisdom from two decades ago to guide your kamo usage. Evoking cannibalism is not always the desired effect!

People will occasionally extend moku by metaphor. moku is at its base a physical word describing a physical act, so these metaphors are secondary, but I've seen moku for "overcome" in some contexts. Like "toki Epelanto li moku e toki Potuke mi" could be a fun way to phrase "Ever since I've started learning Esperanto, my Portuguese proficiency has been on the decline." This is pretty similar to weka, but keep in mind that most instances of moku are simply another angle from some instance of weka. When you eat something, it doesn't exist anymore, at least not in its original form or environment. You're removing it. Reframing a type of weka as a type of moku will frame the act from the perspective of ingesting nutrition and may draw attention away from the removal of that thing.


moli

koro means death, but what does death mean? Sometimes, koro describes a kind of destruction that cannot be undone. Death is permanent. But other times, koro can describe harm or destruction that comes to a treasured object. In these situations, there is still usually a sense of permanency. "rawajisuno korru" would probably be interpreted as "my bed is broken," or even "my bed is broken beyond repair," but probably not "my bed is unclean / I cannot sleep on my bed." That doesn't mean that koro inherently means that there is no possible way to undo the destruction. For example, if resurrection was real, I might still use koro for death anyway. Many cultures believe in reincarnation, life after death, or some sort of returning to nature. Perhaps in these frameworks, koro still describes permanence, but it's not inherently negative.


monsi

hatakanq is the opposite of hataka (face). The semantic space of hatakanq contains things that are behind another thing or the back part of a thing. The caboose of a trains is hatakanq. The space behind a building is hatakanq. A person's back is hatakanq. hatakanq is relative, so its semantic space will always depend on what object it is in relation to. Can also be used as a location, like in 'reoppu sua haterą yaraen hatakanqji' ('I will quickly be coming back').


mu

The semantic space of mu contains noises coming from things being framed as living. A lion's roar is miu. A cough is miu. Humans are animals, but often when they say things, we can understand them, so we might choose to use a different word over miu. But miu is often used to describe languages and vocalizations that are not understood by the speaker. Adjacently, many people describe non-language vocalizations as mu, such as coughs, sneezes, or vocal stims.

By using mu to describe a noise, you are ascribing some level of livingness to the thing that produced it. Using mu to describe a robot's noise makes the robot feel more like a living thing. This can be taken to any extreme, and it often ends up seeming humorous. For example, the sound of some sizzling bacon could be a mu, which is thought provoking. But the sound of a waterfall can also be mu, which is thought provoking in a completely different way. Ascribing qualities of life and animacy to bacon and waterfalls are very different situations. With bacon, it becomes a joke, where the punchline is that mu is an "animal noise" and sizzling is an "animal noise." But with a waterfall, it becomes a deep philosophical musing about what life is.

Using mu to describe the vocalizations of animals is usually a safe bet, but using it in other circumstances can be very profound.


mun

kamà is borrowed from Arabic (qamar), adapted to fit Tawish phonotactics; across the sky are many objects, some of which move quickly or slowly and some of which seem to be fixed in place. The moon, the stars, the planets, even the sun. All of these objects fit within kamà's semantic space, especially the ones that are most obvious at night. If you brought a star down to earth, it would still be kamà. If you left earth and visited a kamà, it would still be a kamà, so mars from the perspective of a rover might still be kamà. The big question here is that to that mars rover, is earth kamà? and then again, to us, is earth a kamà? That's for you to find out.


musi

The semantic space of iyuko also contains all noises and sounds and any act of creating a noise or sound. Any sound from any source can be a iyuko. Music is a iyuko. A scream is iyuko. The sound that a tree makes when it falls in the forest is a iyuko. A sound doesn't have to travel through air to be a iyuko: whale noises are iyuko, and a heartbeat through a stethoscope is iyuko. iyuko could be an absolute pitch, or multiple. It could be sound designed to sound a certain way, or chaotic noise. It is similar to miro but miro carries a connotation of 'reflected,' whereas iyuko does not. Compare: 'sua miru mirolen' (I reflected a sound) v. 'sua iyuku iyukllen' (I played a sound). It may also refer to correctness, reliability, and even health - 'iyukzuna barekunai aiko!' (may your health be blessed! - a greeting). here's a sentence with its 'correct' meaning, 'ryo ni ipaiyuko... iyukrra' (this does not sound right).


mute

see Grammar:13

mute doesn't specify a huge or small number. a few, several, a lot, and a ton are all mute. Usually in context, it's easy to tell these amounts apart. mute as a noun means "amount" and as a verb it can mean either "multiply" or "divide." If I cut a kili in pieces, now I have many kili, and if I cast a spell to multiply a kili into multiple kili, I have many kili, so either way it doesn't matter. Unless it does matter, in which case, ask yourself: why does it matter? The answer to that question will guide you on disambiguating efficiently. mute is used as a general intensifier too when modifying some sort of quality or another modifier.


namako

namà is something extra added at the end. Often while a namà isn't necessary, it's usually a welcome addition, sometimes even desirable. Spices are perfect examples of a prototypical namà. Spices are never the substance of a dish, but they frequently contain a majority of the flavor. a fancy hat or flair is also namà.


nanpa

nampa refers to numbers. Some examples of numbers could be phone numbers, HP in a video game, heart rate, or blood sugar. nampa can also refer to things related to computers, because most things computers do are really lots of little numbers. Some people like to use nampa for math in general, but everyone I know who's into math doesn't like considering all math as numbers. Some math is numbers, and that math can be nampa, but other math isn't. It's your decision where you stand on that. One more thing: nampą (modifier form) can be used to turn words into numbers or to disambiguate words that carry numbers. For example, subeko is infinity - so, for whatever reason, using subeko to mean infinity is ambiguous, subeknnampą would be unambiguous.


nasa

Garibàn comes from the Arabic gharib. The semantic space of garibàn contains deviations from what's considered normal. If most people have blue hair and one person has green hair, that one person is garibàn. If someone grows ten types of herbs and a single carnivorous plant, then that carnivorous plant is garibàn. If most people don't grow herbs, someone who does grow herbs is garibàn. What's considered "normal" here is completely reliant on context. A clown isn't garibàn if everyone around them is also a clown. Nothing is inherently garibàn. The garibàn-ness of all objects will change along with context. the modern spelling leaves the vowel unmarked, so just gariban

I've also seen people use "ike" and "pona" sometimes to modify nasa, clarifying the connotation. Because nasa doesn't have any positive or negative connotations, people whose native languages DO have these connotations associated with weirdness may feel inspired to clarify when the strangeness of something is good or bad. More commonly I see people use "pona" to counteract any assumptions that nasa may be a bad thing in context, but especially in the queer circles surrounding toki pona, weirdness is embraced a lot to the point where this may not be needed. I wouldn't consider modifying nasa like this to be a bad thing! Clarifying connotations is never a bad thing if you feel it's necessary.


nasin

taĉi ties together method and path. Physically, a taĉi is a path or direction one follows, a road you can drive along, any way to reach a location, or perhaps even to wander. metaphysically, taĉi refers to a way to live your life, a way to make something, or any other method. just as easily as main street can be a taĉi, so can communism or the pomodoro technique or setting an alarm or taĉi. It doesn't need a specific destination, but there's usually some purpose to it. it is similar to Japanese 'kata,' like in kamttaĉi (a way of eating), which is a Tawish equivalent of the Japanese tabekata (食べ方).


nena

hana describes parts of a surface that stick out from the parts around it. For example, a button on a computer keyboard could be a hana. A knot in a tree could be a hana. Small toes can be hana. Fingers are probably too long to be hana, but if you use hana for them, you're framing them as bumps as opposed to long objects or a grasping organ. hana can be dull or sharp. A speed bump can be a hana and a hill can be a hana, but so can a spike or a sharp peak of a mountain. Some hana are pleasant to touch, and others might draw blood. If I wanted to make a hana no longer a hana, all I'd need to do is smooth it into the rest of the surface it's part of such that it no longer bulges out.

Another important part of nena's semantic space is its usage for the nose. The nose is a bump, which is why nena can describe it, but as a transitive verb, nena becomes rapidly useful to describe active smelling. Wafting the aroma of roasting chicken can be a type of nena, as an action, but it's drawing attention to the nose. It makes the agent (i.e. the one that smells) a key part of the situation. Note that for most speakers, this usage of nena is not as core to the semantic space. You should still expect to see this usage every now and then.


ni

ni is similar to "this" and "that" and "yonder" in english. it is used in two different contexts. The easiest way to use ni is to point at a physical object. This could be demonstrated with a finger, a gesture of the head, a glance, or even a drawn arrow. In those contexts, the semantic space of "ni" is that thing you're pointing at. You can also use ni as a modifier to be more specific. Are you pointing at a specific box? You can say "poki ni." are you pointing at a specific location? "ma ni" works just as well as "ni." What about the current situation? "tenpo ni" is perfect for this!

extended into the metaphorical, ni can also be used to point at things you or others have said. It always stands in for at least one clause (a clause is any phrase with a verb in it). Most often you'll see it standing in for a previous or upcoming sentence.

ni can also be used as a modifier to elaborate on a word in a sentence. If your sentences are getting too long, ni is among the easiest way to break it up. you can turn "mi wile e soweli pi linja pi suwi mute" into "mi wile e soweli ni: linja ona li suwi mute." Note how "ona" in the next sentence stands in for "soweli ni." If you want to learn more about this, look into anaphora and deixis.

toki pona lacks proximity distinctions, unlike english. In english, the difference between "this" and "that" and "yonder" is how close the object is to the speaker and listener. toki pona's "ni" can fill the meaning of any of these. It is more general, but you can usually tell what it's talking about due to context. If you're worried that people might not be able to tell where something is, you can say how close it is by using "poka" and "weka," among other tools.


nimi

kana are words and names. "lipamanka" is a kana. "paralelepípedo" is a kana. "cupcake" is a kana. This description of kana's semantic space is kana. "kana Xõ / kanaru," likewise, would mean "to apply a name to," similar to "name" (as a verb) in English. You may hear things like "sua kanaru haŝirusun imnyi Álísàn" for "I named my child Alison."


noka

The semantic space of tetenq contains the bottom parts of things meant for touching the ground. Some basic examples of tetenq might be feet or legs. tetenq can refer to the bottom part of a piece of furniture or the wheels of a car. The lowest floor of a building is tetenq. The roots of a tree are tetenq. tetenq can also refer to the action of applying a tetenq to something, such as kicking, stepping on, walking on. If you use tetenq for this, you'll be framing the relationship as some sort of application of a tetenq onto the direct object.


olin

kita describes the act of being emotionally bonded to someone something. In usage, these bonds are typically positive and strong. For example, a very good friend, a partner, a family member, or a pet. Not only could the love for a very good friend be kita, but so can the friend. This can be used with objects too. The bond between many toki ponists and the language toki pona is olin (kita). By using kita, you are evoking some sort of emotional bond. Note that kita doesn't describe all emotional bonds between two things. So if I don't like someone a lot but I still respect them deeply, I could still use kita to describe the respect, but probably not how I don't like them. Also, remember that kita, by default, means 'to like,' not to love. Use kikita for that, and for specific romantic love, use the appropriate grammatical forms.

Some people do actually use olin far more broadly. For example, a minor form of attraction, like enjoying a food a lot. This is less common in my experience, often less likely to be understood, and the attitudes towards this usage are often negative, mostly because it seems like an anglicization. However, some people take it further and use olin to talk about the attraction between magnets, or the delicate bond between math and physics. If you do choose to use olin more broadly, please don't stop where English does and consider taking the meaning a bit farther in a more creative way.


ona

see Grammar:5


open

gaida describes activation. If I activate a light switch, that can be gaida. If I gaida a door, that can be gaida as well. gaida often describes something's function being activated, but it can also describe a beginning of an event, like the start of a concert or the start of a year. Perhaps childhood could be gaida. The throughline here is cause and effect. the act of gaida is always a cause, but gaida can also be the event around that activation. This activation usually isn't a type of creation and serves to exist within the function of the activated thing.


pakala

nameko describes the act of destruction. nameko is often not intended, for example a mistake. The thing being destroyed were your expectations. Sometimes, when people's expectations are destroyed, they say "nameko" by itself to describe said destruction, similar to the way expletives work in other languages. This isn't something special that nameko does, but nameko is used this way because the situations we swear in are often situations where we've made mistakes, or when something has been destroyed. nameko can also be intentional, such as smash therapy or intentional use of explosions. nameko can also describe harm. If something cut my skin, I might use nameko to describe that. In fact, nameko merges the ideas of harm and destruction. In English, these concepts are given separate words that are used based on the animacy of what they describe. Note that you can't use nameko for a general accident or to signal unintentionality, it will always carry a sense of destruction with it. 'nameksun' would not mean 'my mistake;' rather, it would be closer to say ~'my destruction / the ruin brought about by me / the pain I caused intentionally or unintentionally.' you could say, 'subeko no ryo ni nameksun ana~' (all of this was my fault) but it isn't exactly the same, like if you say 'kohanamekã,' that doesn't mean serendipity or accidental gift, it means destructive gift or mistaken gift, which is rather rude to say.


pali

irime refers to work, specifically jobs, activities, or deeds. it refers to labour, development, or implementation, but you can't use it to mean projects, composition, or of the like. Irime comes from the Arabic word 'ilm (knowledge) but over iterations of the language and due to the appearance of shire (a modal, also meaning knowledge), irime semantically drifted to mean work instead. In technical contexts, irime can refer to operating something (working something), but this meaning is also valid in romantic, even sexual, contexts. It can refer to crafting or a craft, like mahokairime is magic-craft (e.g., witchcraft), but don't ever say mahokairimemas (magic crafter? magic worker?) to mean magician, use mahokamas for that.

pali is, at its core, a transitive verb. Therefore, as a noun, pali is something that has been made or worked on. This could be a specific project, document, device, invention, practice session, job, task, etc.


palisa

The semantic space of maku contains things all things that are longer than they are wide that are not flexible enough to be bent significantly. any maku will resist bending and either break or only bend slightly under significant pressure. If a maku is looked at from a different perspective where there is enough power to bend it, it might not firmly sit within maku's semantic space. A good test that works most of the time is to check to see if it’s flexible enough to be bent into a 0 degree angle or if it’s flexible enough to be tied in a knot, if it is, then it’s probably not a maku.

Taughtness is an interesting thing to think about with palisa. I used to sail a lot, and some ropes (lines, we called them) were always taught and effectively served the same function as any poles we could grab onto. These were called "shrouds," and I would definitely have used palisa to describe them, even though in their relaxed form they would be as pliable as any rope. So when something becomes reliably taught enough, you can use "palisa" to describe it.


pan

roĉi is a starchy staple food such as rice, wheat, barley, teff, potato, and corn. roĉi can also refer to products made from these, such as breads and porridges. more specific examples include injera, fufu, pasta, tteokbokki, tortillas, cakes, congee, cereal. many speakers will use roĉi to describe corn products like corn flakes but won't use it to describe corn on the cob.

A less common usage of pan refers to legumes as well as grains, as well as their products such as tempeh, natto, and tofu. This is less popular because legumes are usually seen as a "protein," and are less starchy than the above examples. But legumes are indeed still high in starch, so especially when dried, pan can make sense.

other plant-based foods are almost never called pan unless they resemble a food usually made of grains, like a zucchini pancake might still be pan even though it doesn't have any grain in it. foods with a type of pan as their primary ingredient can be called pan as a whole, such as (japanese style) poke, ramen, gyro, or a burrito, even if they have a lot of non-pan components.


pana

The semantic space of denh contains all acts that set objects in motion. This can be a type of emission. The act of a fire giving off heat it denh. The act of a child laughing is denh, because the child is emitting laughter, setting it in motion. Throwing a baseball, hitting a hockey puck, and kicking a football (the kind that Americans call a "soccer ball") are all denh. Defenestration is a type of denh, because you are setting something in motion (out a window). the object that does the act of denh doesn't have to set something in motion away from itself. Buying a piano online for a friend is denh, or at the very least the act of having the piano be delivered is denh. Less physical objects can be the subject of denh too: knowledge, advice, or good feelings (bad ones, too!). examples: 'soppai denh asaharumajinh' (he autodefenestrated / he delivered himself out of a window). See Grammar:14 for more information.

pana as a noun is used to mean either the act of setting something in motion, or something that has been set in motion. So a gift is a type of pana, and so is an offering, a package, a ball I have thrown, etc.


pilin

nuko is an experience of sensory qualia. nuko can refer just as easily to internal senses as it can to external ones, so nuko can mean both touch and emote. most speakers use nuko to mean "opinion" as well, framing an opinion as some sort of internal sensory experience.


pimeja

The semantic space of kuru contains types of darkness. This could talk about specific colors like deep red or dark emerald green, but it can also talk about shadows, places where there is less light, or the absence of brighter colors. kuru can describe anything that is dark. From some perspectives, the night is a type of darkness more than a period of time (just as a day can be a type of brightness and a year can be a type of circle around the sun).


pini

pini is the moment something comes to a close. this can be as simple as a door shutting or a light turning off, but it can be more abstract, like a department at a college fizzling out. pini has no connotations of irreversibility, unlike "pinis" in its language of origin (tok pisin), which can grammatically specify irreversibility.


pipi

ẑupi tend to be on the smaller side. They can have legs, and their legs will be under them. They usually have six or more legs if they have any at all. When they move quickly, they will usually move in straight lines, whether that be across a surface or through the air. Sometimes, they don't have legs. ẑupi tend to be squishy inside, and if there's a hard part it will be on the outside. This is similar to the concept of an exoskeleton.

pipi Kewapi has at times emphasized insignificance as a big part of pipi. We kill pipi. Perhaps as a group pipi are important, but, and I quote, "a single pipi is not a loss." This means that if one pipi were to die, it wouldn't affect much. I recommend experimenting with what can or can't be a pipi.

Note that pipi don't have to be terrestrial! They can fly or be aquatic. There's significant overlap between pipi and both kala and waso. A butterfly is a waso and a pipi, where a shrimp is both a kala and a pipi.


poka

ga is at its core is anything, either an object or location, that is nearby or to the side of something else. This could be the space next to someone, or at the side of someone, such as the lefthand side of a walking person. But this can also be part of an object, such as someone's hips or a car door. Extended into the metaphorical space, ga becomes extremely useful for talking about relationships. people and objects that one has relationships with are in proximity with them, so one might describe a friend as ga and a loose acquaintance as garra (not daiki). two things don't have to be physically next to each other in order to be a ga. here is one example: 'denh sulla tsamaeu gazun' (give, to me, the soup that is to your side.'


poki

yowa are containers. They're meant to contain things. Bags, boxes, and bins are great examples of yowa. Furniture that contains things, such as trash cans, drawers, shelves, and even closets, are a big part of yowa. Some yowa might be metaphysical rather than physical. All semantic spaces of all toki pona words can be framed as yowa, containing possible meanings. You could look at labels through this lens, and people who choose to use a label are placing themselves within the semantic space of said label. This usage of yowa is fairly common. To explore yowa's semantic space more, click here.


pona

ii is any good quality. it can be modified to specify which but otherwise, ii's semantic space is huge. you can sometimes even use it to mean help, like in 'sua ieiiru so' (I bettered him), which could be translated as 'I made him better (i.e., helped him).' you can just use the word Tawa has for help though, that, too, is fine.


sama

see Grammar:9.4


seli

naŝita is heat. If something's hot or giving off heat, it's naŝita. the heat itself is also naŝita. this word can also be used to talk about spicy food through metaphor because spicy food literally makes your mouth feel warmer, but be careful not to calque english by explaining that if you use naŝita like that. Household appliances used to heat food can be naŝita, like a stovetop. naŝita can be used as a verb to mean "cook," if in english you see cooking as always some sort of application of heat. Chopping up vegetables to put in a raw salad isn't a good example of naŝita.


selo

kugi describes the outer layer of an object. It doesn't matter what the object is. For example, skin can be kugi, and bark can be kugi. This can get extended into the metaphorical. What are outer layers for? Usually, they protect that which is inside. Perhaps an attempt to ignore bigotry could be described as a kugi. But in doing so, you're framing it as an outer layer of your mind. kugi for "protect" has a different vibe when compared with kiwi's protect meaning. With kiwi, a protection is an act of maintaining, perhaps from afar with no risk to yourself, but with kugi, protection always puts something at risk, be it the skin of an apple, the walls of a castle, or the case of a guitar.

selo don't always have to contain anything important, even though they usually do. Balloons are usually empty (save for air) and they can still be selo. In fact, a vacuum chamber's walls could be selo, even though they literally have nothing inside of the (save for "dark energy" or whatever don't @ me I'm not a physicist). As a verb, selo can mean "to surround fully," or "to become the outer layer of (something)." Some people use this for hugging, which is very fun.


seme

the semantic space of fuyu is unknown. That's the whole point! When one uses fuyu, it's unclear what it means. It is an invitation for an answer. this is very similar to wh- words in english like "what" and "which" and "who." unlike english, Tawa only has the one (fuyu). If you want to specify what your query is about, you can modify a different word with fuyu. "fuyu  miyoĉ hyo?" can mean "which plant?" for example. You can also modify fuyu with another word for a broader meaning. "fuyumiyą" can only be a type of miyo, but "fuyu miyo" can be anything related to any plant. Often, fuyu is a direct prompt to the listener, but it's frequently used as a prompt to the self or an expression of a lack of knowledge. speakers sometimes ask a question with fuyu only to answer it.


sewi

sora embodies a very powerful conceptual metaphor that exists within toki pona. In toki pona, upwards and high things and divinity are connected. These are two separate ideas that sewi connects by referring to both of them. Things that are religious or sacred can be sora. sora on its own can easily refer to that which is divine or worshipped. But sora very simply talks about the space above something. Keep in mind that all of these examples can work in the other sense of sora. can be the top painting of two paintings, and "sora hon" can be someone who just climbed a big mountain. And of course, "sora taka" could be the divinity of a rock.


sijelo

gaya is the substance of something. it's the stuff. we're all made out of physical matter, and gaya is that matter and the material it's made up of. Many speakers use gaya as a modifier to mean "physical." For example, "unsu gayaji" (present physically) could mean "irl" (as opposed to something like "unsu twanaji" (present electronically) to mean "online.")


sike

inspired from the Urdu "bhola," baka represents round objects and cycles. baka can be used to refer to the smoothness of an edge. baka can be used to talk about marbles and flat disks such as cookies. baka can talk about wheels or balls or even gyroscopes. Because baka can also represent cycles, it can be used to describe a repeated action. A for loop (in computer coding) is a baka. "baką fofu" (circular talk) could be repeating the same thing over and over again. "baką ŝahada" could be a never ending cycle of war, or some online discourse that just won't end. Whether it's physical or not, a baka will always loop around and end where it started. Baka can also refer to someone innocent or their innocence (e.g., virginity) and can even refer to a fool. Baka was inspired by bhola in Urdu (no influence from Japanese 'baka') and it is purely a coincidence that it has come to mean 'fool.' examples: 'a! baką haŝirulen' (what an innocent child!), 'zu ni bakalen monh a ŝirera hahasunǫ' (you are an idiot because you did not understand my joke).


sin

aŝi means "new" or "again." It's almost always clear from context which of those two aŝi means. I recommend paying attention to aŝi's usage in the wild to try to figure out if it's ever ambiguous between new and again. The reason why it isn't is because the difference between those two concepts isn't very far. It doesn't matter if you've done it before. In toki pona and Tawa, something can be new even if it isn't the first time it's happened. examples: 'sua kiwinq irimesunǫ ŝi aŝi' (I finished my work, yet again). It is recommended to use 'ze' for again instead of aŝi, so it would be better written as 'sua ze kiwinq irimesunǫ' (I, again, finished my work).

sin can mean a lot of things, and that range of possibility is given by explaining what something is new to. children are new to this world, toki pona learners are new to toki pona communities, a new brand of soda is new to the Free Market Economy which we all Love So Much, etc. These can all be sin!


sina

sina is the listener, reader, the one who interprets what the speaker says, or any group that contains them. Similarly to mi, when quoting others, it doesn't necessarily refer to the one who is listening at the moment, and instead refers to the one listening from when the quote is from. But besides situations like that, sina always includes the listener. Many people also use sina as a general hypothetical pronoun, similar to "one" in english when used as a pronoun. This may be due to Anglophone influence and many speakers completely abstain from this usage, prefering "jan" or "ijo." Others use sina when talking to inanimate objects. Perhaps this frames the object as listening. I recommend playing around with this at least a little bit.


sinpin

sinpin refers to objects that face directions. walls can easily be sinpin, and so can human faces or any of the front part of an animal. the part of a building that faces the road can be sinpin. So can any other walls of the same building, though. By using sinpin to describe a surface, you're drawing attention to the directionality of it. When one rolls a die, perhaps the most crucial sinpin is the one facing up. At an art museum, even if a painting were to be placed on the ground face up, sinpin is still the best word to describe its physicality because the concept of using it as one would use a supa is disrespectful to the artist and the artwork. While sinpin is usually used for vertical surfaces, it is these examples of usage that restrict me from describing it as strictly such. sinpin is in contrast with supa.

sinpin also refers to the space that something else faces. "sinpin tomo" can mean both "front surface of the house" and "the place in front of the house."

sinpin can't be extended into a metaphorical space to talk about time. "tenpo sinpin" might mean future or past to people from different cultures, so in order to use it like that you'd need to build it up by explaining the metaphor. For example, if you framed yourself as facing the past and walking backwards, you could even use "tenpo sinpin" to talk about the past!


sitelen

emono are depictions, such as images or symbols. A painting or a photograph are both emono. It's also common to see emono used to describe a symbol in a writing system. The letter Q and the maya glyph for b'alam (jaguar) are both emono. Even three dimensional objects can be emono, like a scale model of a boat of a model of architecture in a modern art museum. emono is usually used for things that were created, but these don't need to be organized. A random scribble is still a emono even though it doesn't necessarily represent anything. pure random noise has the potential to be a emono too, but doing so will likely frame the noise as having some sort of order to it. perhaps it was chosen? maybe it's being used for something? maybe it's a mistake? explore emono!

Ask yourself: is static a type of sitelen? When does randomness become something we can see as made with intent? Must we name something for it to have meaning to us? If nobody looks at a picture, is it still a sitelen?


sona

The semantic space of ŝire contains all knowledge. If something is knowable or known, it is ŝire. For example, the knowledge of time of day can ŝire. Wisdom can be ŝire. ŝire can also be a skill. The knowledge of how to write a book can certainly be ŝire. Knowing about a person or who they are can be ŝire. A person's memory can be ŝire. If I were to describe information on a computer as "ŝire," I would be framing it as being known by the computer. 'knowing someone' could be translated as 'ŝiren honlen' (to know someone). For its modal usage, check Grammar:16.


soweli

'fihon' describes creatures with several distinctive traits. fihon tend to be fluffy. fihon tend to be larger than a plum. Their legs tend to be under them, and they tend to have four of them. When they want to move quickly, they run on their legs, with moments where all legs are off the ground. They are warm to the touch.


suli

poki is big, tall, wide, vast, deep, etc. Any physical dimension of largeness can be poki. toki pona doesn't use different words to distinguish different ways of being big. In the metaphysical world of toki pona, if something is big that means it is important, so poki can also mean important. poki could mean that something is made up of a lot of stuff, often implying that it's complex. poki can talk about necessity. If something is crucial, it is poki. Combining poki with perspectives can show what people value, allowing us to construct a map of what matters more or less to others.


suno

sowo is an object that emits light or the light itself. It can be brightness or a shimmer or a sparkle. the sun is a sowo, a lamp is a sowo, but sunlight and lamplight are also sowo. many people use sowo by itself to mean a day cycle, framing a day as a type of light. this kind of framing is interesting because it doesn't sit right with all speakers, but it's understandable, so speakers can learn more about how each other see the world this way.


supa

jirei permeate all of our lives. notice all the flat objects around you. you use them every day. you sleep on one, you work on one, you eat at one, you cook at one. one important aspect of jirei is that things can rest upon it. here's a good test you can try out to tell if something is jirei: if I were to start tilting a jirei on its side ever so slowly, it would cease to be jirei as soon as things fell off of it. That means that a surface with very high friction (like felt) can continue to be jirei for longer than something with very low friction (like a flat sheet of teflon). if you have stuck things to a surface and turn it completely upside down, it can stay a jirei.


suwi

sawa describes pleasant aspects of senses. If something is pleasant to the mouth, i.e. it is sweet, that is sawa. If something is pleasant to the eye, i.e. cute, that is sawa as well. The scent of apples might be sawa as well. A soft or fuzzy texture can be sawa. A pleasant sound can be described as sawa. sawa can also describe other pleasant things, such as actions or people. Like if someone bakes you a pie, not only is the pie usually sawa, but so is the act of making it. Note that sawa isn't used for savory pleasant flavors much, usually just sweet ones. 


tan

tan is any reason. it can also meany why, etc.


taso

watadà is not like the English 'but.' its semantic scope allows it to exclude other items. for example: 'sua watadà kita zuǫ' (I only like you), 'iiwatadą hon ni foŝsun' (good-only people are my friends)


tawa

tawa is motion. it is anything that moves. it can refer to progression (like in 'ii miri Tawa' (~good medium for progressing)). it can even be used as a modal (tawaũ) to mean 'going [to]' or 'from the perspective [of],' but this is an archaic usage. in Modern Tawa, you would better elaborate, and only use tawaũ as a last resort. so, 'progress is good from his perspective' could be 'tawatei ni ii solla' (as for progress, it is good to him). you wouldn't use it in phrases like 'fofunaithu honlós gasonllanh' ((I) heard people from his side).


telo

ĉi can be liquids and other things that don’t hold a shape. Water, milk, and vinegar are ĉi. ĉi can be more viscous. Oils are ĉi. ĉi can be used less literally, to describe things that aren’t physical objects. The changing aspect of a way of speaking Tawa that doesn’t stay the same can be ĉi. If something changes form and doesn’t hold its shape, it fits within ĉi’s semantic space. For semi-fluids, like custards, it is better to use kara.


tenpo

Wano is a garibankana, so it does not follow regular inflection particles. It never inflects. wano + -óy would always be wanoóy and wano + -ã is similarly wanoã. as for the semantic space: you'll usually hear people say that wano means "time." but what does that mean? what even is the abstract concept of time? this definition isn't very useful, so a different angle I suggest people take is to look at wano as either a situation or a duration. wano could be that one time I robbed a bank, or all the times I cooked with shmalts. wano could be a minute, or an hour, or an eon (1 billion years). wano can be the time when the sun shines, or the cycle it takes for the earth to rotate around the sun, or for the earth to rotate such that the sun goes away and comes back. wano can be the time when it's dark, the time when it's cold or warm, the time it takes for the moon to go through all its phases, the time it takes for markets to set up and disband a few times a week, the time when we work, the time when we sleep, the time when we travel, or the time when we arrive. wano can talk about the abstract concept of time, but usually it is used to talk about specific events, situations, and durations. the o is never omitted for wano.


toki

The semantic space of fofo contains any form or act of communication. This can be communication using the mouth, such as spoken language, or communication using the hands, such as signed languages. This could be communication using a written medium. fofo doesn't have to just be for humans. Bee dances are fofo. fofo doesn't have to be between two parties either. Communication with one's self (such as someone thinking or talking to themselves) is fofo. fofo can get very abstract. If I can feel the atmospheric pressure change and I can detect that it's probably going to rain later because I'm old, I could say that the sky is fofo-ing to me (soral ni fofu su). If I read a sign, the action that sign is doing can be fofo. Don't use 'fofo' as a generic replacement for 'hi' or 'hello,' though. Also, you can't use 'fofo' to generically mean communicate - for that, waka is better.


tomo

An asa contains objects meant to contain things being framed as living. Sometimes living is literal. A family of five is considered living, so their domicile can be considered an asa. Sometimes living is a metaphor. A dollhouse can be considered an asa too!


tonsi

tonsi describes any divergence in gender from the expectations of a european patriarchy. "European patriarchy" is different from "gender binary," and that's important here. Binary trans folk are the genders that they are, fullstop. That fact does not prevent patriarchal society from marginalizing them, especially recently. tonsi describes this and many other forms of gender based marginalization.

For this reason, cis people who don't conform to patriarchal gender roles often fall under the umbrella of "tonsi." tonsi is a very wide umbrella, like all toki pona words, and typically includes nonbinary people, gender nonconforming people, and binary trans people, though usage varies from person to person (see below).

People may choose for themselves if they (as an individual) exist within its semantic space, much like the other gender words in toki pona. tonsi, mije, and meli are not mutually exclusive at all. one can be one, two, all three, or none, or even something else, if they can come up with it! Sonja Lang has described it as a grassroots community made word.

Many ask, including myself: can tonsi be used to describe binary trans people as well? Here are some notes on usage, based on survey with a sample size of about 300, including 50 binary trans people:

  • 7/10 binary trans people use "tonsi" to describe themselves

  • 7/10 binary trans people use "tonsi" to describe binary trans people, in general

  • only 1.8/10 binary trans people are upset when others use "tonsi" to describe them

  • 3.5/10 binary trans people believe that "tonsi" should NOT be used to describe binary trans people as a group. 2.5/10 didn't care one way or the other. 4/10 believe that "tonsi" SHOULD be used to describe binary trans people as a group.


It's always good to check before calling someone "tonsi" directly, but know that most binary trans toki pona speakers won't be upset when called "tonsi." As for binary trans people generally or as a group, this usage is controversial among binary trans people. Use this data to guide your own usage. "should" has a slight edge on "should not," especially considering that the people who don't care are likely fine with the usage and just won't advocate that others SHOULD use it. A few respondents contacted me to share this sentiment.

A notable bias here is that a transmedicalist who believes that this usage of tonsi is transphobic sent the form out in her circles multiple times, encouraging people to "reject tonsi." I have done nothing of the sort to try to skew data either way.

The form is closed. The sociology of gender, transphobia, etc around this is not something I will get into here for brevity, so enjoy these cold heartless numbers. I will release a new form soon.

tonsi is one of the only words on this list that is not featured in Toki Pona: the Language of Good by Sonja Lang. It's considered important because toki pona has ways of talking about men and women, but no easy way to talk about nonbinary people. This is why I included it. If you look up frequency of usage, frequency for tonsi may be lower than some other words that are not featured in Toki Pona: the Language of Good by Sonja Lang. This is because many speakers don't use tonsi, meli, or mije. If a speaker uses meli and mije, they will almost definitely also use tonsi from time to time.


tu

zu is, at its core, an act of separation. in Tawa, if there are two of something, one is framing them as separate. you don't need to modify a word with zu in order for there to be two of it, so using zu is saying something: I am separating these two things. "zu eauca" can evoke an importance between two heads of lettuce. zu is used frequently as a verb to mean "cut" or "divide." it can be used for cutting something into more than two pieces because zu doesn't specify a specific amount of times. like "sua zureu karabazun" could describe four separate cuts, which could be up to 16 pieces! zu can also be metaphysical, describing divorce or any type of distance between people or objects with enough context. some other good examples of zu are splitting up a class into two groups or drawing the boundaries between two subparts of a large piece of land, potentially in a colonial context. NOTE: zu is typically marked when used as a verb or the accusative is placed directly after it to ensure there is no ambiguity.

unpa's semantic space has to do with sex and sexual activity. The number of participants doesn't matter; solo masturbation is equally as valid to describe as unpa as an orgy, as is anything between. Kink can be a part of unpa, but it is specifically framed as a part of sex, whether in the lead-up as in foreplay, in the act, or any other part of sex. For comparison, horniness and other feelings or concepts related to sex are generally not called unpa, but they are often considered in their relationship to sex. Additionally, sexual positions and sexual preferences are generally not unpa, as they are not the act. However, they may be framed in their relationship to sex, such as by use of the word unpa as a modifier.

Gender and biological sex do not play a role in unpa's semantic space; put another way, unpa doesn't change when the identities of those involved change. It is still unpa.

Reproduction also generally does not play a role in unpa's semantic space, a feature it shares with the word "sex" describing the act but not reproduction. Reproduction may be referenced in relation to unpa, such as when discussing breeding animals, but unpa generally does not imply or refer to reproduction on its own. There are caveats to this however; some speakers will use unpa to refer to pollination, one of the means by which plants reproduce. By the use of unpa, this is being framed as a sexual act at the same time as an act of reproduction; this is by analogy to sex, as pollination is the closest similar thing that applies to plants.



uta

harana can refer to the mouth, tongue, or kissing. in some contexts, it may be used to describe specific types of kissing, like french kisses.


utala

The semantic space of ŝahada contains conflicts, contests, and battles. When ŝahada is used, it’s talking about some sort of struggle or competition between two or more parties, or something being framed that way. So it could be a battle between two rival armies, but it could also be a battle between me and myself if I’m framing it that way. ŝahada is often targeted, closer to “attack” than “battle.” Anything can be an ŝahada if it is being framed as a struggle or attack of some sort that involves two parties. Playing a musical instrument could be an ŝahada for a beginner player. Taking a test is a very fun thing to call an ŝahada, because the test-taker is fighting the test, like in 'raŝimassta′a ŝahada payalǫ' (the student fought the paper). martyr also fits in the scope, like in 'soa ŝahadathu irimesonji' (he gave his life for his work (i.e., he died a martyr amidst his work - metaphorical, not literal martyrdom; however, if the person really is dead, could be literal)).


walo

ohira is any bright color like white, pink, light blue, etc. it can also refer to milk.


wan

It is best to think about wan as first and foremost some type of union. other objects are coming together into one whole. That kind of motion is core to the word "wan." the number meaning is secondary. wan is used frequently to mean combine, marry, mix, overlap, etc. using wan as a modifier can indeed mean that there is only one of something, but it's always good to think about it as a single unified whole. it can also mean specific, like in 'wan tan,' which can mean 'one reason' or 'specific reason' depending on the context!


waso

ngoshi are creatures. they almost always have wings, and when they want to move quickly they use those wings to propel them forward. They frequently have feathers. They have two legs that they use to walk on land. ngoshi usually lay eggs and often (but not always) sing. many speakers use waso (ngoshi) to refer to things like planes that aren't living creatures, which has been especially common in my experience with toki pona meetups, where the flight itself and the plane are both called ngoshi. This usage is controversial though. ngoshi can be used to refer to birds that don't fly or use their wings to fly, such as ostriches, kiwi birds, or penguins, and doing so shows the way you split up the animal kingdom. there are quite a few speakers (including myself) who don't use waso (ngoshi) for these creatures, and that shows the way we split up the animal kingdom too. This semantic space is based off a pilot study I conducted with a sample size of 117. I will publish the results in a paper if enough people bother me.


wawa

moto is qualities of power and strength. A very strong person is moto. The strength required to talk to new people one has never met before is moto. moto focuses less on the ability or possibility itself and more on the power and motivation behind it. moto belongs to things with agency. If something is enabled, it is moto. For example, if a lightbulb has been turned on, it becomes moto. moto is used frequently to refer to energy itself, both in an abstract sense (such as spoons or motivation) and in a physical sense, such as electricity or force. Many people aren't moto until they drink their morning coffee. The coffee makes them moto.

Another great angle of "wawa" is the concept of saturation and concentration. For example, a strong drink (as in, one that has a lot of alcohol) is wawa, but as you dilute it with water, it becomes less wawa. Another example is paint. Saturated paint mixed with a neutral shade of paint (like gray, black, or white) will slowly and slowly become less wawa. Depending on how salty water is, one could describe it as wawa, for example if comparing saltwater fish to freshwater fish.


weka

fiyui describes distance. It is similar to other location words in that it can describe a place, but instead of describing a specific relation to proximity, it describes the lack thereof. Hence, fiyui describes a place that is not nearby. This could be outside a house or simply outside of reality. It can describe the act of disappearing or vanishing. Transitively, it can mean the act of removal or stealing. fiyui can also be more passive as an action, such as ignoring, just as easily as it can be an active action like getting rid of something.


wile

The semantic space of subo contains all desires. A desire to eat is a subo. A desire to be near others is a subo. These desires can be influenced by external forces: a desire to do tedious chores motivated by the prospect of negative consequences is a subo. By using subo to describe something, it's being framed as a type of desire. For example, if I normally don't want to tidy up my workspace but I feel obligated to anyway, if I used subo to describe that feeling of obligation, I'd be describing it as some sort of desire. This can be used for anything, no matter how animate. Speakers frame non-living objects as having desires very frequently. P.S. see Grammar:16 on more information regarding subo.

A warning I have for wile is assuming it contains the idea of a "need." A "need" is a complex concept and trying to use wile to describe it is misguided. While most needs can be framed as types of desires, the goal of "wile" isn't to merge those concepts, it's to throw one of them away. This enables a toki ponist to align their desires with their actions by giving them a word to describe their desires without any complex connotation. Sometimes it's more useful to say "my body wants food, but I don't want to eat."

Another part of wile's usage is its use as a preverb. wile changes the sentence it's part of to make the subject desire to perform the verb, rather than just performing it. It's similar to the word "want" in english.


foŝo

foŝo is a versatile and gender-neutral word that functions similarly to the English slang 'bro' or 'blud.' its semantic scope encompasses any individual with whom one has an understanding. 'sa ni foŝssun' (she is my 'foŝo') or 'sua watadą kiwinqthu zuǫ monh zu ni foŝssun' (I only stopped you because you are my 'foŝo').


yupekosi

it refers to anything absurd, from haha (laugh) + kari (make). it literal means 'laugh-produce'.

jasima

miro carries a connotation of 'reflected,' whereas iyuko does not. Compare: 'sua miru mirolen' (I reflected a sound) v. 'sua iyuku iyukllen' (I played a sound). Miro can be used to describe mirrors or resounding. it can be used to describe reflections or reverberations. it is anything that echoes. it can also be used in the sense of mirroring (duplicating) or copying, just as a mirror 'copies' you. it can also refer to döppelgangers or mirror images. it can also mean a place full of sound. it can also describe an idea, message, or reaction spreading widely and strongly. it also has an internal meaning: something deeply affects people or 'rings' emotionally (e.g., 'her words mirrored/resounded with(in) the audience). sometimes, it can also mean undeniably loud, like a 'hakunqmimirã' (loudest lie) would be a lie that's really hard (maybe even impossible) to deny. if a name or event 'resounds' or 'echoes' throughout the world or a place, it can describe fame (locally or globally) too.


tenpo suno

So can any other walls of the same building, though. By using hataka to describe a surface, you're drawing attention to the directionality of it. When one rolls a die, perhaps the most crucial hataka is the one facing up. At an art museum, even if a painting were to be placed on the ground face up, hataka is still the best word to describe its physicality because the concept of using it as one would use a jirei is disrespectful to the artist and the artwork. While hataka is usually used for vertical surfaces, it is these examples of usage that restrict me from describing it as strictly such. hataka is in contrast with jirei.
